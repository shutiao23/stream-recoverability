## Abstract

*Two short paragraphs (written draft; ~240 words total).*

The practical question posed by a missing stream-temperature record is not which reconstruction model is most accurate on average, but whether reconstructing a given gap with a given model beats the default handling the downstream user would otherwise apply. We define realized benefit as the difference in mean absolute error between the default handling (calendar-day climatology fill) and the recovery model on identical artificial gaps, and we measure its duration structure first. Benefit declines monotonically with gap length on both outcome-disjoint panels: mean benefit per station–gap unit falls from +0.98 °C (first panel) / +0.91 °C (second panel) at 7 d to −0.89 / −1.50 °C at 180 d, and the share of units harmed by reconstruction rises from 0.0–0.4% at 7–30 d to 27.4% / 46.8% at 90 d and 83.6% / 95.6% at 180 d. The log10-interpolated zero crossing of the unit-mean benefit relative to climatology fill sits at 105 d on the first panel (95% network-cluster bootstrap CI 98–114 d) and 91 d on the second (95% CI 85–95 d) on the fitted 7/14/30/60/90/180/365-d grid; downstream thermal-regime metrics flip earlier — annual mean at 40–55 d, with metric-family medians at 39–60 d. Reconstruction over climatology fill is therefore defensible only for gaps well below the MAE-scale crossing — of the order of one to three months on the MAE scale and less on thermal-regime scales. These intervals contain the m_max = 49-day threshold that Johnson et al. \cite{johnson2021datagap} derived from different stations, a different method, and a different target metric: their value falls inside our downstream 39–60-day window and at roughly half our MAE-scale crossing.

Which gaps inside a duration are worth treating is then a ranking problem — and ranking by model error is the wrong instrument, since the legacy risk curve correlates *negatively* with realized benefit at 90/180 d (−0.19/−0.54). A differential stress curve — fitting-period benefit scored by the very model it ranks — ranks realized benefit positively within duration strata (placement-level Spearman 0.297 / 0.303 / 0.327 / 0.371 / 0.556 at 7/14/30/60/90/180 d, second panel) and beats the strongest station-history baseline in 10 of 12 paired cells — all six second-panel cells — with the two crossing cells both first-panel. At within-stratum top-20% budgets it realizes +1.22 / +1.09 / +0.84 / +0.63 / +0.38 / −0.64 °C at 7/14/30/60/90/180 d — margins of +0.22 / +0.28 / +0.24 / +0.32 / +0.32 / +1.01 °C over the gap-length policy, with per-duration harmful rates 9.4% / 8.4% / 10.9% / 14.7% / 25.1% / 62.7% — and at 180 d, where the model is on average worse than climatology, it selects a least-harmful fifth (−0.64 °C, 63% harmful) rather than treating by duration (−1.65 °C, 86%) or by risk (−2.88 °C, 98%). The evaluation design — outcome-disjoint networks, nested temporal hold-out, trivial baselines, ranking metrics with clustered intervals, and decision accounting — is a contribution in itself relative to the closest recent work (Table 1). The confirmatory protocol is registered externally [protocol v5, `paper/v14_plan/protocol_v5.md`].

## 1. Introduction

*(Written draft prose; bullets only where marked.)*

**Para 1 — the decision problem.** A failed logger can leave weeks or months of missing stream temperature while boundary observations, neighboring stations, and forcing records remain available, and the manager must act before the missing truth arrives. The manager's question is not which imputation method is best on average but which action is best for this outage geometry, this recovery model, and this downstream workflow: how large an error the intended reconstruction will make on a specific future gap, and whether acting on that expectation is better than applying the default handling — leaving the gap empty, filling it with a climatological expectation, or interpolating across it. The relevant quantity is therefore not predicted model error but predicted **incremental benefit over the default handling**:

`B = MAE(default handling) − MAE(model)` per gap, positive when reconstruction beats the default. Error is a property of the model, the gap geometry, and the default; benefit is a property of the *difference*. This paper develops and prospectively evaluates an estimator of B: a differential historical stress test in which identical artificial gaps are cut into the already-observed fitting years of the record and scored with both the intended recovery model and the default handling, so that the resulting benefit curve inherits both error structures and the difference between them.

**Para 2 — the prior work we no longer claim.** Our earlier record (\cite{pfister2017debates} labeling; negative-result spin-off `paper/v14_plan/negative_result_note.md`) established two facts that motivate the change of estimand. First, local recovery difficulty persists: fitting-period block stress curves ranked a model's future reconstruction *loss* on outcome-disjoint networks at pooled Spearman 0.945 (network-level 0.80 at directly supported horizons). Second, that ranking carries no increment over a station's own history and does not triage: the station-by-horizon historical mean of the same fitting record nearly matched it (0.942 pooled; paired network difference +0.042 with 95% CI [−0.0006, +0.1154], straddling zero), and the loss-based predictor was the worst non-random fixed-budget policy in a captured-loss experiment (CapturedLoss@20% 0.337 vs 0.512 for simple descriptors). We therefore **no longer claim loss ranking, rank-magnitude superiority over station history, or loss-based triage in any form**; the v13 record is published as the negative result it is, and this paper predicts benefit instead of loss.

**Para 3 — the signal is in the default's difficulty.** The failure of the loss-based design was structural, not statistical. Loss of a given model is largely determined by observables the station history already encodes (station, gap duration), whereas benefit is the difference between default difficulty and model difficulty — the signal lives in the *subtrahend*, which loss-based stress tests never measured. Development-corpus diagnostics on the archived thermal-metric run of the first-panel networks (`results/revision_v12/t08_downstream_metrics/agent_a/placement_thermal_metrics.csv`, n = 1,965 placements at 7/30/90 d) showed that a risk score ranking model MAE correlated *negatively* with realized benefit (pooled ρ = −0.230; −0.370 at 90 d alone) while ranking the climatology default's own MAE correlated positively (+0.659), and the two predictors correlated at only 0.304. A leave-one-out benefit curve — a feasibility upper bound, not a forward test — ranked realized benefit at ρ = +0.363, with top-20% mean realized benefit +0.992 °C against +0.615 °C panel-wide and a harmful-treatment share of 6.9% vs 37.7% (PLAN §2 F1–F3): the estimand, not the machinery, was the problem. P0 (this paper's differential stress test on two panels) was the forward, outcome-disjoint test of that claim, and a sanity check confirms the mechanism is the difference: the differential prediction and the legacy risk prediction correlate at −0.722 on the second panel (first panel −0.647; `sanity_checks.txt`), i.e., the new instrument is nearly the complement of the old one.

**Para 4 — related work and the incremental claim.** Gap-filling studies compare reconstruction models on imputation quality, and flux-network studies use stratified artificial gaps to show uncertainty grows with gap length and season (\cite{richardson2007longgaps}); none ties a pre-outage score to the realized benefit of a specific model over a specific default on unseen networks. The closest direct competitor for stream temperature is Johnson et al. \cite{johnson2021datagap}: 40 stations, gaps of 0–357 d in 7-day steps with 100 random start dates each, and operational thresholds (m_max = 49 days) for annual mean, phase, and amplitude recovery. Our increment over that work is deliberately **not "more accurate reconstruction"**: it is that (i) benefit is scored *model-conditionally* — the curve is built with the very model whose gaps it ranks, and (ii) the evaluation is *benefit-oriented* — against the default handling, with harmful treatments counted, not against raw imputation error. The verification literature supplies the decision framing we adopt: continuous cost–loss value curves \cite{laio2007verification}, climatology as the reference strategy \cite{mason2004climatology}, relative economic value with realistic threshold loss \cite{richardson2000economicvalue}, \cite{roulin2007skill}, and the response-effectiveness chain \cite{verkade2011floodwarning}, \cite{khader2013voi} [see Discussion]. On the methodological side, the four closest recent evaluations — Husic et al. \cite{husic2025interrogating} (interpretable machine learning for model diagnosis), Pechlivanidis et al. \cite{pechlivanidis2020keydrivers} (drivers of seasonal forecast quality, WRR), El Ouahabi et al. \cite{elouahabi2026multisite} (multi-site uncertainty prediction), and Auer et al. \cite{auer2024uncertainty} (information needed for uncertainty prediction) — each develop ranking or uncertainty tools, but none combines all five evaluation-design features we treat as minimal (Table 1). **Roadmap sentence (required by REVIEW_v14.md Major 5 / WORKPLAN_v14.md §1.3):** consistent with the operational hierarchy of the questions, the Results of this paper lead with the duration structure of realized benefit — when reconstruction is worth doing at all (§3.1) — and only then turn to ranking (§3.2–3.4: why model-error ranking is wrong, the differential stress test as the main ranking result, and the decision layer) and to boundaries (§3.5).

## 2. Methods

*(Subsections 2.1–2.7 are written draft prose; 2.8 is a structured description without numbers.)*

### 2.1 Study phases and data

Daily mean stream temperature is the target. The development collection combined 55 river networks, 217 stations, and 1,260 station-gap units. The first confirmation panel (42 networks, 1,440 station–gap units; 25,848 directly supported evaluation placements at 7/14/30/60/90/180 d: 4,380/4,380/4,380/4,380/4,380/3,948 over 42/42/42/42/42/39 networks) was used for method development under the benefit estimand and is reported descriptively; its networks derive from the route-A confirmation roster (`results/development_v11/route_a_confirmation/qualified_panel.csv`) and span USGS HUC-8/HUC-10 catalog networks and European providers (ARSO Slovenia, GKD Bavaria, LUBW Baden-Württemberg, RWS Netherlands, FOEN Switzerland). The second, outcome-disjoint panel (57 networks: 32 US, 15 Czech, 10 Norwegian; 1,446 units; 26,258 directly supported evaluation placements at 7/14/30/60/90/180 d: 4,480/4,480/4,480/4,480/4,400/3,938 over 57/57/57/57/56/53 networks) provides this paper's prospective evidence; no second-panel network appeared in any earlier outcome set (rosters: `results/development_v11/second_confirmation/frozen_scoring_roster_v2.csv`, `results/development_v11/second_confirmation/scoring/placement_losses.csv`). Calendar years are ordered; the first 70% are fitting years and the remainder supply observed evaluation truth. For each network, seasonally stratified artificial gaps (7/14/30/60/90/180 d; DJF/MAM/JJA/SON; up to 20 placements per station-gap-season cell) are replayed from the archived geometry; the 14/60-d fitting rows were cut in the round-2 revision with the identical archived machinery (`scripts/152_run_v14_r2_fitting_cuts_14_60.py`, probe reruns bit-identical to 8.9e−16; REVIEW_v14_round2.md Major 2): every gap scored with the recovery model is scored identically with the default handling. The primary loss is mean absolute error (MAE, °C). A station–gap *unit* is (network, station, gap length); a *placement* is one seasonally placed artificial gap of that unit. The unit-level and placement-level aggregation conventions and their reporting roles are specified in §2.6.

### 2.2 The differential stress design

A second chronological split nests inside the fitting years: the first 70% of those years train the recovery model (gradient boosting, 300 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.05, local gap boundaries and synchronous donor temperatures — the prespecified condition), and the remaining fitting years supply observed artificial-gap truth for both the model and the default. The default handling is calendar-day climatology: for each calendar day the median of that day's values over the inner-fit years, estimated under exactly the model's training mask. Because the default is estimated only inside the inner-fitting split, its parameters contain no information from the evaluation period; this is the design's central no-leakage rule — a benefit curve built with a default that had seen evaluation truth would be an oracle, not a screen. The replay of archived geometry is literal: the differential scoring uses the same placement rosters (station, gap length, season, start) on which the model's fitting losses were originally scored, so benefit is defined placement-by-placement as

`B(p) = MAE_default(p) − MAE_model(p)` (°C),

positive when reconstruction beats climatology on placement p, and harmful when negative. The benefit curve is the four-level groupby-mean of these fitting-period benefits (§2.3); it inherits the model's own error structure and the default's own error structure, and the difference between them. **Every comparison in this paper is paired on identical placement subsets**: the two predictors' predictions are evaluated on the same placements of the same networks, and inference resamples networks jointly for both sides of each paired difference (canonical implementation `results/v14/p0_differential/paired_diff_vs_r6.csv`, seed 20260903, 2,000 network-cluster draws per (panel, duration) cell). ### 2.3 Support hierarchy and the four-level fallback

Every benefit prediction carries a support tier built by the same fallback ladder used for losses, applied generically to the benefit column: exact station × gap × season curves of the network's own fitting-period benefit; station × gap with season collapsed; network × gap with station collapsed; and the network-mean fallback. A placement whose finest available tier is the network mean is flagged unsupported and reported separately (P0 `unit_predictions_*.csv` `diff_supported` column). The four tiers delimit the claim: ranking strength is expected to degrade monotonically with distance from the exact cell [TODO: reproduce the v13-style tier accounting for benefit curves; see v13 §3.2 for the loss-side template]. Durations outside the fitted set {7, 14, 30, 60, 90, 180} d and the 365-day extrapolation boundary are handled per the support-or-abstain rule of protocol v5 §1.3 (never extrapolated without real same-horizon fitting support; 365-d benefit exists in the archived evaluation tables but has no direct fitting support and enters only as a labeled boundary row in Table 2).

### 2.4 Comparators and the no-cross-duration-pooling reporting rule

Five comparators are scored on the identical placement subsets. (i) Gap-length-only ("duration-only"): because budgets are fixed within duration strata, gap length is constant within a stratum and this comparator is realized by a frozen random order within ties — its treated set is therefore a random draw from the stratum and its expectation is the stratum mean; this is the honest within-stratum reading of "rank by gap length". (ii) Random. (iii) The v13 risk curve (model-MAE ranking), imported unchanged — an adversarial comparator whose predictions must bit-match the archived predictor (P0 verification: max |risk − archived| = 8.88e−16 over the 874 direct units, `sanity_checks.txt`). (iv) r6-diff, the station × horizon historical mean of fitting-period *benefit* (the strongest fitting-record baseline under the new estimand; r6-diff is constant within every (station, duration) cell and therefore cannot rank placements inside a cell — §2.6/§3.3). (v) Default-difficulty, the climatology-MAE curve alone (P1: at short gaps it was the strongest single loss-side predictor of benefit; §3.5). (vi) The **negated legacy risk curve** (−risk): the same bit-identical v13 predictor with its sign flipped. Because Spearman is odd under monotone sign change, ρ(−risk, benefit) = −ρ(risk, benefit) exactly; the comparator is not a new model but the reviewer-inevitable question of whether the differential machine is needed at all — whether model-error magnitude already signals harmfulness once its sign is flipped (§3.3; paired placement-level deltas and bootstrap CIs in `results/v14/p0_differential/paired_diff_vs_negrisk.csv`).

*Methodological literacy (kept and tightened).* We report ranking statistics **per duration stratum and never pooled across durations**, and we report **differences, never ratios against near-zero denominators** (WORKPLAN_v14.md §1.4; the archived cross-duration "17.6×" ratio is voided in `results/v14/p0_differential/GATE_REVISION_v2.md`). Pooling placements across durations inflates Spearman because gap length dominates benefit scale — on the P0 second panel the direct pooled placement ρ (0.547; pooled duration-only 0.516 on the same rows — duration-confounded, Appendix B) exceeds every per-duration ρ (0.297–0.556) — and pooling lets gap-length baselines absorb the treatment effect: at a cross-duration top-20% budget the duration-only ranking and the differential curve are nearly tied, while within-duration strata the differential curve leads at every duration (§3.4). Cross-fold and cross-stratum pooling of rank and AUC-type statistics is a documented source of bias and irreproducibility in classifier and regression evaluation \cite{forman2010apples}, \cite{airola2011auc}; we adopt the same discipline for budget statistics. The pooled-direct numbers therefore appear only in Appendix B, always beside the duration-only value and always labeled **duration-confounded, reported for comparability only**.

### 2.5 Inference, intervals, and the n < 15 rule

The river network is the primary extrapolation unit; all predictions and comparisons leave out complete networks, and cross-validation honors the network structure of the data \cite{roberts2017crossvalidation} — a requirement for transfer claims over space and time \cite{wenger2012transferability}. Results report per-duration placement-level Spearman and within-stratum budget means as the primary scale (§2.6), and network-level Spearman (over network means of predicted and realized benefit) as a secondary scale. All network-level correlations and all paired differences (two predictors on identical subsets) carry two-sided 95% network-cluster bootstrap intervals: 2,000 draws resampling networks with replacement within provider strata (US/CZ/NO on the second panel), preserving achieved stratum composition. Placement-level pooled statistics that accompany budget rows are computed within a duration stratum only. *New subsection (WORKPLAN_v14.md §1.4; REVIEW_v14.md Major 1–2, Minor 2).* Five norms are binding throughout. ### 3.2 Why ranking by model error is wrong

*All numbers at placement-within-duration level, the paper's main level (§2.6).*

A manager whose operational default is climatology needs to know not how hard a gap is for the model, but whether the model beats the default on it — and ranking by model error answers the wrong question. Three layers of evidence make the wrongness concrete. First, on the prospective second panel the legacy v13 risk curve (the archived model-MAE predictor, imported bit-identical, §2.4) correlates with realized benefit at placement level only weakly at short durations and **negatively at long ones**: ρ(risk, benefit) = +0.184 / +0.129 / −0.192 / −0.540 at 7/30/90/180 d (`results/v14/p1_baselines/metrics_table.csv` placement_pooled risk rows; equivalently P0 `metrics_by_horizon.csv`). The same sign pattern predates this paper: on the archived development-corpus run over the first-panel networks (1,965 placements at 7/30/90 d, `results/revision_v12/t08_downstream_metrics/agent_a/placement_thermal_metrics.csv`), risk ranked realized benefit at pooled ρ = −0.230 and at 90 d alone ρ = −0.370 — independent evidence, produced before the differential design existed, that model-error ranking is anti-correlated with benefit exactly where the default starts to matter. Second, the mechanism is visible in the realized errors: ρ(realized model MAE, realized benefit) is near zero at short durations and collapses to ≈ −0.92 at 180 d on both panels (first −0.054 / −0.126 / −0.551 / −0.918; second +0.038 / −0.097 / −0.543 / −0.918 at 7/30/90/180 d; computed from the archived per-placement scoring tables `results/development_v11/route_a_confirmation/placement_losses.csv` and `results/development_v11/second_confirmation/scoring/placement_losses.csv`) — once model error grows large relative to the default, the model's own error predicts its harmfulness, not its benefit. Third, the two error structures are positively coupled: ρ(model MAE, default climatology MAE) = 0.316 / 0.496 / 0.362 / 0.276 (first) and 0.459 / 0.578 / 0.425 / 0.226 (second) at 7/30/90/180 d — **gaps that are hard for the model are hard for the default, so error magnitude carries almost no information about the difference**, and the difference is the object a treatment decision needs. The pooled risk figure from the archived gate record (−0.481, placement level, second panel, direct pooled) is **duration-confounded** — on the same rows the pooled duration-only baseline reaches 0.604 (`p1_baselines/metrics_table.csv` direct rows) — and never appears without that label (Appendix B, with the demoted old-G2 diagnostic it produced).

**The sign-flipped legacy predictor: the method's irreplaceable range is the positive-benefit regime (7–60 d).** Because Spearman is odd under monotone sign change, ρ(−risk) = −ρ(risk) exactly; the negated v13 predictor is therefore a free comparator that any reader can construct (§2.4). It fails exactly where the differential curve's contribution lives and nearly ties where it does not. The mechanism behind this boundary is developed in §4.2; its consequence for emphasis is that the paper's central ranking claim is the short-gap one (7–60 d, the regime where treatment is on average beneficial, §3.1), with 180 d carrying the harm-avoidance claim (§3.4) rather than a ranking claim no other instrument could make.

**The paired test against the strongest fitting-record baseline (the redefined G2).** Table 3 pairs the differential curve against r6-diff — the station × duration historical mean of fitting-period benefit, the strongest baseline a fitting record can offer under the new estimand. **Why r6-diff cannot win this test, structurally (the mechanism paragraph of the paper's methodological claim).** r6-diff is the station × duration historical mean of fitting-period benefit; inside a (station, duration) cell it is a single constant, so it cannot rank that cell's placements at all, and its placement-level ρ comes entirely from between-cell differences. The differential curve's entire placement-level increment is therefore *inside-cell seasonal ranking* — exactly the content the old loss-based estimator lacked (REVIEW_v14.md §1.2). The season structure is measurable (`results/v14/p0_differential/season_signal.csv`): within-cell (network × station × duration) season η² of the fitting-period model error grows from 0.091 to 0.395 (first) and 0.129 to 0.455 (second) across 7 → 180 d, and the evaluation-period realized benefit shows the same growth (0.206 → 0.389 first; 0.213 → 0.476 second). The share of within-cell variance attributable to season rises with duration, and 180 d — where the paired increment is largest — is where the seasonal structure is strongest. In the old estimator (model error), seasonal variation was swamped by station and duration effects; in the new estimator (benefit), seasonal variation is the signal itself — which is why the v13-era season-stratified loss curves "bought nothing" while the benefit curves buy the paired increment of Table 3.

**The aggregation diagnostic (SI).** REVIEW_v14.md §1.3 records that the adversarial reviewer first tested the paired difference at the station × duration unit level, found it collapsed, and identified the level itself as the error. The diagnosis is confirmed and documented in `paired_diff_vs_r6_unit_diagnostic.csv` (SI-Table U1 / SI-Fig U1): aggregating placements to unit means erases the seasonal increment — the unit-level deltas collapse to ≈ 0 or negative (first +0.009 / −0.012 / −0.002 / +0.011; second −0.002 / −0.000 / +0.000 / +0.032 at 7/30/90/180 d on the original grid, the 14/60-d rows in SI-Table U1; e.g., first-panel 30 d Δρ = −0.012, 95% CI [−0.035, +0.002], P(>0) = 0.045) with 11 of 12 unit-level CIs straddling zero, while the placement-level deltas of Table 3 exclude zero in 10 of 12 cells. The sole unit-level survivor (second-panel 180 d, +0.032 [+0.001, +0.071]) is also the cell with the strongest seasonal η², consistent with the mechanism account. Tests of the seasonal increment must therefore be run at placement level, within duration strata (§2.6); the unit-level rows are the paper's own demonstration that the wrong level erases the finding.

### 4.1 The Johnson bracketing: an interval statement on independent evidence

The duration result of §3.1 is the paper's most actionable output, and its strongest external support is the bracketing of an independent published threshold. Johnson et al. \cite{johnson2021datagap} systematically cut gaps into 40 stations and applied threshold-based recovery criteria for annual-mean/phase/amplitude metrics, deriving m_max = 49 d; we use two outcome-disjoint multi-provider panels, a model-vs-climatology difference in MAE, and ten downstream thermal metrics. Their threshold falls inside our downstream family-median window (39–60 d, Table 6) and at roughly half our MAE-scale crossing (105 d [98–114] first / 91 d [85–95] second). Convergence of this kind means more when the studies differ in everything except the phenomenon: the statement "recoverability is lost somewhere between roughly one and two months, and earlier for thermal-regime metrics" is now supported by two independent operationalizations, and it is the sense in which the duration result is a property of stream-temperature records rather than of our pipeline. The purposes differ in a way worth stating: their m_max is a conservative "heed the data gap" criterion (a reconstruction that merely degrades slowly still counts as recovered), while our crossing is a treatment rule against a specific default; both counsel against reconstruction beyond ~1–2 months on the metric scales they measure, and neither supports automatic filling at longer horizons. Where the two studies could diverge — and would be informative if they did — is under a different default handling: our §4.3 shows the rule is default-conditional by construction. An earlier point-claim of convergence on an unfounded ~60-d internal estimate is withdrawn (REVIEW_v14_round2.md Major 1): the interval statements above are the claims, and both are backed by the named artifacts of §3.1.

### 4.2 Why model error sign-flips into harm at long gaps but not into benefit at short ones

The −risk analysis of §3.3 delimits where the differential machinery is needed, and the boundary has a mechanism. Realized benefit is B = MAE_default − MAE_model; ranking B within a stratum requires predicting the *difference*, and a predictor of MAE_model alone can rank B only where model error dominates that difference. The dominance holds at long gaps: the realized-error correlation ρ(realized model MAE, realized benefit) is near zero at 7/30 d and collapses to ≈ −0.92 at 180 d on both panels (first −0.054 / −0.126 / −0.551 / −0.918; second +0.038 / −0.097 / −0.543 / −0.918 at 7/30/90/180 d; §3.2), and the legacy risk predictor — a model of that same error — correlates −0.19/−0.54 with benefit at 90/180 d. Once model error is large relative to the default's error, error magnitude *is* harmfulness, and sign-flipping the error ranking suffices: ρ(−risk) reaches +0.373 / +0.541 at 180 d against the differential curve's +0.308 / +0.556 (Table 3). At short gaps the same errors are small and positively coupled across the two sides of the difference (difficulty coupling ρ(model MAE, default MAE) = 0.316–0.578 at 7/30 d; §3.2), so B is dominated by fine-scale structure — season of onset, local boundary conditions — that an error ranking cannot see and that its sign flip cannot recover: ρ(−risk) is *negative* at 7/30 d (−0.134 / −0.044 first; −0.184 / −0.129 second) while the differential curve, which inherits both error structures and their difference, ranks positively (0.265–0.303). The same mechanism explains the duration dependence of the seasonal signal: within-cell season η² of the fitting-period benefit grows with duration, and the differential increment is carried by seasonal placement structure that only a difference estimand preserves (§3.3). The v13 error predictor was therefore not "almost right": it was right about harm at long gaps — where realized benefit is negative anyway and the treatment decision is whether to treat at all, not which gaps to prefer — and wrong about benefit at short gaps, where the treatment decision is live and only the differential curve ranks.

### 4.3 Default dependence, downstream metrics, and the linear-interpolation finding

The duration rule is a statement about the climatology default, and §3.5's default-difficulty parity already shows that the default carries most of the short-gap signal. P4 makes the dependence explicit with a third default — linear interpolation between the two flanking observed days, which is also the model's own boundary feature: **the linear ramp is the model's minimal sub-model** (`results/v14/p4_downstream/REPORT.md` §1). Two findings follow. First, versus the linear default the model is beneficial at *every* horizon on both panels: benefit over the linear default is positive in 98 of 100 panel × horizon × metric cells (the two non-positive cells are phase_doy, −0.06 d first-panel 7 d and −0.02 d second-panel 30 d; `results/v14/p4_flip_analysis/flip_analysis.md` §5) and grows with the horizon — the model learns to avoid the drift error that the linear ramp compounds. Second, the linear default itself becomes worse than climatology from ~90 d up: err_linear − err_clim is positive for 10/10 metrics at 90 d and 10/10 at 180/365 d on both panels (annual mean +0.114 / +0.147 °C at 90 d, +1.151 / +1.370 °C at 180 d), while at 7 d linear is the better default for all metrics (flip_analysis.md §5.2). **The choice of default reverses the direction of the headline**: under the climatology default, reconstruction is net-harmful from ~1–3 months up on the MAE scale and every downstream metric crosses into net harm by 180 d (first panel) / 90 d (second); under the linear default that crossing does not exist within 7–365 d, because reconstruction beats the linear ramp everywhere. The duration rule is therefore conditional on the default the user would otherwise apply — which is precisely why benefit over a *named* default, not model error, is the estimand, and why reporting norm (v) of §2.7 requires the default in every claim. No-fill remains the worst default wherever it is computable (annual mean at 180 d, first panel: no-fill error 2.80 °C vs 1.50 °C for linear; P4 REPORT §4.3).

### 4.4 Decision framing: continuous cost–loss and why we report curves, not one number

We frame the downstream choice in the continuous cost–loss tradition of Laio & Tamea \cite{laio2007verification}: normalized loss ρ_ξ(x, χ) = |χ − x| + 2(ξ − 0.5)(χ − x) with a single cost-ratio parameter ξ = c/l, the climatology-mean forecast as the reference whose expected cost is the mean absolute deviation δ, and the optimal decision at the quantile χ* = P⁻¹(1 − ξ). The appropriate object is the curve EC(ξ)/δ over ξ — with the hydrologically realistic region ξ < 0.5 highlighted — not a single skill number, because the identity ∫₀¹ EC(ξ) dξ = CRPS shows that a single average weights the unrealistic region ξ > 0.5 equally with the decision-relevant one. §3.4 reports the empirical curves (below 1 at every ξ at 7/14/30/180 d and pooled; bounded excursions peaking at EC/δ = 1.038 (60 d, ξ ∈ [0.40, 0.60]) and at the named 1.033 (90 d, ξ ∈ [0.45, 0.60])). For the same reason we treat selective-prediction summaries (risk vs coverage; E-AURC normalized between random and oracle \cite{geifman2017selective}, audited per \cite{traub2024overcoming}) as the reporting standard for any abstention claim, and we position the conditional-covariance operator — previously demoted in our own record — as the theory-optimal rejection baseline \cite{zaoui2020rejectoption} rather than as a failed mechanism story (§A.2).

### 4.5 Defending the value framing: the Laugesen critique

The flexible-value literature \cite{laugesen2023flexiblevalue} argues that classical relative economic value (a) assumes risk-neutral users, (b) handles only binary decisions, (c) overstates value through binarization, and (d) presumes users can self-calibrate thresholds perfectly before outcomes — making envelope curves optimistic. Our defense is structural rather than rhetorical: the primary presentation is the continuous EC(ξ)/δ curve (§3.4), which by construction avoids (b) and (c); we cite \cite{laugesen2023flexiblevalue} explicitly in discussing (a) and (d); and we label every value quantity in this paper as *potential* value — the value a fully responsive user would realize — never as realized value. The binary REV-style supplement \cite{richardson2000economicvalue} is reported only with the fixed-suboptimal-threshold value-loss accounting of \cite{roulin2007skill}, because the threshold a real user will hold is the realistic number — and that number is negative at 7/14/30 d with τ = 0 (−0.003 / −0.054 / −0.138, §3.4).

### 4.6 Response effectiveness: the η chain

Benefit in the field is not the MAE difference but the MAE difference multiplied by the chain of response effectiveness: whether downstream users actually consume reconstructed values, and whether the distortion would have changed their conclusions. Three independent strands of the forecast-value literature make response effectiveness the dominant uncertainty — larger than forecast skill itself: the DEFRA value chain La = Lp × R × Pa × Pr × Pe with realized value near 0.5·Lp \cite{verkade2011floodwarning}; compliance-probability analysis in which value of information falls as compliance falls \cite{khader2013voi}; and loss-reduction coefficients by action class whose sensitivity dominates the value calculation (Pappenberger et al. 2015/JRC EUR 27533 facts, per PLAN §5.5). We therefore write the downstream benefit of treating gap g as B(g) = [MAE_default − MAE_model](g) × η, with η ∈ [0, 1] an explicit response-effectiveness multiplier, and we report sensitivity envelopes over η rather than point estimates; §3.4 gives the η = 0.5 anchor numbers, and the full envelope over η ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} is in SI (`eta_sensitivity.csv`).

### 4.7 Honest reporting of the partial band, harmful-rate margins, and benchmark brackets

The placement-level ρ of the differential curve sits inside the 0.25–0.35 partial band at 7/30 d (0.297/0.303 on the second panel; 0.265/0.302 first) while network-level ρ clears the confirmatory band (0.718/0.551); both numbers are reported, with their correct reading. The band reflects the small absolute spread of realized benefit at short durations — the model beats climatology on ~90% of 7-d gaps, so there is little benefit variance left to rank — and it is precisely the regime in which no alternative instrument can rank benefit at all: r6-diff is constant inside (station, duration) cells (§3.3) and the sign-flipped legacy predictor ranks with the *wrong sign* (§3.3, §4.2). The 7/30-d strata are therefore the method's strongest cells, not its weakest: modest absolute ρ with irreplaceable content beats high absolute ρ that a free sign flip can reproduce (180 d). This positive reading supersedes the earlier self-description of the band as thin marginal information (REVIEW_v14_round2.md Major 3). Every budget claim carries its harmful rate; per-duration harmful rates (9.4%/10.9%/25.1%/62.7%, §3.4) are the reporting unit, and the archived cross-duration 14.5% figure is not used as a headline (§2.7, Appendix B). The station-history near-tie that ended the v13 loss claim has a constructive reading in the benchmark-bracket tradition \cite{seibert2018upperlower}: the upper benchmark is the benefit model fitted on the target's own future real gaps (an undeployable oracle), the lower benchmark is the pooled global benefit curve, and every deployable predictor is reported by its position between them — under the benefit estimand, §3.3's per-duration network ρ (0.647–0.718) sits close to the season-LOO feasibility upper bounds (0.240–0.734 per duration; 0.668 pooled direct; `season_loo_upper.csv`), and Table 4 gives the same brackets in decision units. This reframing turns the v13 null into the boundary condition of the design rather than its epitaph.

### 4.8 Limitations

(i) The 7/30-d placement-level ρ values are modest in absolute terms (0.25–0.35); their irreplaceability is argued from the paired comparisons against the sign-flipped legacy predictor and r6-diff (§3.3, §4.2, §4.7), not from the absolute level; (ii) the benefit estimand is scored against climatology as the primary default — under the linear-interpolation default the direction of the headline reverses (§4.3), and this default-dependence is a property, not a bug, but it bounds every operational claim to the named default (norm (v), §2.7); (iii) the deployed recovery model is a frozen gradient-boosting configuration — the three-family robustness check of §3.1 (Table 7) shows the duration rule is not specific to that configuration, but trivial-fill and process-based (air2stream/Mohseni) rosters were not executed in this revision and no robustness claim is made beyond the tested families and their archived scoring tables (§2.8); (iv) the 365-d horizon is excluded from deployable ranking claims for lack of real same-horizon fitting support; (v) the stationarity boundary of §3.5 (T2 +0.005 [−0.007, +0.017]) means no drift-protection claim is made; (vi) the η chain is unmeasured — envelopes only (§4.6); (vii) the P3 agent-script loss and re-implementation is recorded in the SI reproducibility statement; (viii) evidence-role labels: every internal-panel number in this paper is exploratory/descriptive relative to protocol v5's prospective panel — the internal second panel is hash-bound but not externally verifiable preregistration (§Open Research); the negative-result spin-off (`paper/v14_plan/negative_result_note.md`) carries the v13 claims we no longer make, and \cite{pfister2017debates} labels each evidence role, including the gate revisions of Appendix A.8.

## 5. Conclusions

[Bullet skeleton — final sentences fixed now that P1–P4 numbers exist; evidence roles per claims_v14.md.] (1) *Duration rule (headline):* realized benefit over the climatology default declines monotonically with gap duration on two outcome-disjoint panels (+0.98/+0.91 °C at 7 d to −0.89/−1.50 °C at 180 d, first/second), the harmful-unit share jumps from 0.0–0.4% at ≤ 30 d to 27.4%/46.8% at 90 d and 83.6%/95.6% at 180 d, and the unit-mean benefit over climatology fill crosses zero at 105 d [95% CI 98–114] (first) / 91 d [85–95] (second) — reconstruction over climatology fill is a defensible default only for gaps below the MAE-scale crossing, and downstream thermal-regime metrics flip earlier (family medians 39–60 d); the rule is robust across three structurally different recovery-model families (Table 7). (2) *External bracketing:* Johnson et al.'s independent m_max = 49 d \cite{johnson2021datagap} falls inside our downstream 39–60-d window and at roughly half our MAE-scale crossing. (3) *Ranking (second layer, irreplaceable at short gaps):* the legacy loss-based risk curve ranks benefit with the wrong sign at 7–30 d and 90/180 d (90/180 d: −0.19/−0.54); simply negating it nearly ties the differential curve at 180 d (0.541 vs 0.556, second panel) but ranks with the wrong sign or not at all in the positive-benefit regime (−0.184/−0.172/−0.129/+0.024 at 7/14/30/60 d vs +0.297/+0.319/+0.303/+0.327), where the differential curve is the only tested instrument that ranks benefit; it also beats the strongest station-history baseline (r6-diff) in 10 of 12 paired cells with 10 of 12 CIs excluding zero — all six second-panel cells pass. (4) *Decision:* within-duration top-20% budgets over the climatology default realize +1.219/+1.086/+0.840/+0.629/+0.380/−0.636 °C at 7/14/30/60/90/180 d — margins over duration-only +0.22/+0.28/+0.24/+0.32/+0.32/+1.01 °C, harmful rates 9.4%/8.4%/10.9%/14.7%/25.1%/62.7% — with EC(ξ)/δ < 1 at every ξ at 7/14/30/180 d and pooled, and bounded excursions (≤ 1.04) at 60/90 d. (5) *Harm avoidance:* at 180 d, where the model is net-harmful against the climatology default, benefit ranking selects the least-harmful fifth (−0.636 °C, 62.7% harmful vs −1.647 °C/85.5% duration-only, −2.881 °C/98.2% risk). (6) *Boundaries:* default-difficulty reaches network-level parity at 7/30 d (0.722/0.562 vs 0.718/0.551); the drift-stratum advantage is not significant (T2 +0.005 [−0.007, +0.017]) — the method requires approximate stationarity. (7) The evaluation design (Table 1) is a contribution over the closest recent work. No automatic filling or station-removal policy is supported by the present evidence; treatment decisions require the within-duration, harmful-rate-aware, default-named reading of §3.1–3.5.
