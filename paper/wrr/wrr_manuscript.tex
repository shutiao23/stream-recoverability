\begin{abstract}
River-temperature archives support assessments of sustained thermal extremes, yet record completeness does not determine whether those extremes remain recoverable. Here, we use \RSourceYears{} complete reference station-years, paired gap experiments and frozen tests on new European station histories to examine the annual hottest consecutive 30-day mean (H30). We separate the effects of missing-window support, diagnostic target and calibration context. For equal gap length, moving a 30-day gap into the climatological peak raises climatology-based H30 absolute error by \RPosition{}\,$^{\circ}$C; extending a fully hidden peak also increases error. H30 diagnostics improve screening relative to daily diagnostics: local recalibration on Swiss records yields \RTestRetTargetDailyGain{} additional retention points [\RTestRetTargetDailyLow{}, \RTestRetTargetDailyHigh{}]. Further peak-window summaries do not establish a stable five-point advantage over strong direct H30 uncertainty references, including matched failure-probability learners and a developed conditional distribution. The frozen window rule retains \RTestRetFrozenRetention{}\% of \RTestRetCases{} Swiss cases but incurs \RTestRetFrozenRisk{}\% failures against a 5\% calibration target. Local recalibration lowers observed risk to \RTestRetRecalibratedRisk{}\%, although seven test stations cannot validate a population guarantee. Numerical screening and hot-year classification also rank policies differently; inexpensive unknown answers can outperform the original screen. These findings support target-specific assessment of thermal archives while identifying calibration transport, conditional distribution quality and intended use as separate limits on record fitness.
\begin{plainlanguagesummary}
A temperature record can contain many observations and still miss the weeks needed to estimate a river's annual thermal extreme. We test which incomplete years can support the hottest 30-day mean. Diagnostics aimed at that statistic retain more usable estimates than diagnostics of daily reconstruction error. Adding a more detailed description of the uncertain hottest period gives no consistent further advantage over strong direct diagnostics. A screening rule calibrated on existing rivers also exceeds its target error rate when transferred unchanged to new Swiss records. Local examples improve the observed result, but too few independent rivers are available to establish a reliable population error limit. Finally, reporting an accurate temperature, identifying a hot year and withholding an answer have different consequences. Archive assessment therefore needs to specify both the quantity being reported and the evidence supporting its use.
\end{plainlanguagesummary}
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
River-temperature records describe thermal exposure and climatic extremes and support assessments of aquatic habitat \cite{caissie2006}. Many of these applications depend on sustained warm conditions rather than individual daily temperatures. The annual hottest consecutive 30-day mean, H30, captures one such property. Recovering it from an incomplete record requires information about the warmest part of the year: high annual coverage can coexist with an unobserved thermal peak, whereas a substantial cool-season gap can leave that peak well constrained. The scientific question is how the location and persistence of missing observations affect the information available for a specified thermal statistic.

Previous studies establish that missing-data timing affects annual stream-temperature analyses \cite{johnson2021} and that seasonal information can support thermal-peak reconstruction from heterogeneous records \cite{beaufort2022}. This work provides a basis for assessing incomplete archives beyond a single coverage threshold. It leaves a further comparison to resolve: whether diagnosing error in the intended statistic adds useful information beyond daily reconstruction diagnostics, and whether explicitly representing its uncertain peak window adds a further benefit. These are distinct questions. A comparison that changes both the diagnostic target and its window representation cannot attribute improvement to either component.

The distinction matters because several diagnostics can encode the same seasonal support. Calendar position, historical peak timing and disagreement among reconstructed H30 estimates may already describe much of the information carried by an explicit overlap variable. Furthermore, the true peak window is unavailable when a record is assessed. Its location can be used to diagnose reconstruction errors in a complete reference record, but a deployable rule must depend on visible observations and permitted history. Matched comparisons are therefore needed to separate the value of the target statistic from the value of a particular representation of its support.

A useful diagnostic must also retain its meaning across rivers and uses. Selective prediction provides established tools for exchanging the number of returned estimates against their error risk \cite{geifman2017,angelopoulos2025}. However, a threshold calibrated under one mix of reconstruction inputs and river conditions need not attain the same risk after transfer. Stream-temperature models with similar accuracy within their development environment can respond differently to environmental change \cite{topp2023}. A further distinction arises between numerical accuracy and classification: an H30 estimate can exceed a numerical tolerance while retaining the correct hot-year label, or cross a classification threshold despite a small numerical error.

We combine complete reference records, paired missing-data experiments and frozen new-station tests to address three questions. First, how do gap position, duration and persistent reconstruction error propagate into H30? Second, how much screening information comes from the diagnostic target, and how much from additional peak-window summaries? Third, which calibration and use conditions preserve the value of that screening?
