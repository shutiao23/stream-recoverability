\begin{abstract}
Thermal archives are screened by completeness thresholds, assuming that how much
data is missing decides whether an annual statistic can be trusted. That
assumption has never been tested against the statistic's own support, because a
gap's length and its position cannot be varied independently in a record that
failed on its own. Here we combine 5114
complete river station-years from 146 networks with 51524 designed outages, vary
the two properties of a gap separately, and score admission rules on held-out
networks and on sealed panels that took no part in development. We find that the
error of a hottest 30-day mean grows $9.6\times$ along the position axis and
$3.5\times$ along the length axis, whereas an annual mean reverses the two
($1.5\times$ and $10.2\times$): the same gap is destructive or harmless depending
only on which statistic is being computed. Screening station-years by the share
of that statistic's support a gap hides cuts consequential errors on a
retrospective network holdout from 9.7\% to 2.5\% at 70\% retention ($-7.1$
points, $[-9.0,-5.4]$), by $-7.9$ points
($[-10.2,-5.8]$) on 39 sealed USGS networks and by $-4.6$ ($[-6.5,-3.3]$) on a
pre-registered multi-agency panel of 32 networks, yet by only $-0.7$ to $-1.9$
points on the gaps those same networks actually experience. Completeness
thresholds are therefore the wrong axis for extremes and the right one for means;
what an agency can bank today is the smaller empirical-arm gain, and that
difference is what its own gap calendar decides.

\begin{plainlanguagesummary}
Rivers are getting warmer, and managers ask how hot a river got last summer. The
answer needs a year of daily temperatures, but records have holes, and the usual
screen counts how many days are missing. We show that this is the wrong question
for a summer peak: what matters is whether the missing days include the few weeks
the peak depends on. Using complete years from 146 river networks, we place gaps
of identical length in different positions and compare what two statistics lose.
A gap over the peak window destroys the hottest 30-day mean while the same gap in
autumn barely moves it; a whole-year average behaves in the opposite way. A rule
that screens on this "hidden support" instead of on gap length removes most large
errors on held-out and sealed networks, but when we look at the gaps these
networks really experience the gain shrinks to a fraction. Results about exact
crossover days, autumn maintenance benefits and "peak never observed" counts are
exploratory or use proxies and should not yet guide operations.
\end{plainlanguagesummary}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cold-water habitat assessment, thermal-regime classification and
criterion-exceedance accounting all reduce a year of daily water temperature to
one number --- a hottest 30-day mean, an annual mean, a seasonal amplitude
\cite{caissie2006,isaak2017} --- and it is that number, not the daily series,
that enters a management judgement. The error that matters is therefore the
error of the derived statistic. On the panel assembled here, computing the
summer peak from recorded days alone, which is the default once a station-year
passes a completeness screen, carries a mean absolute error of
\textbf{1.305\,$^{\circ}$C}, or 1.23 year-to-year standard deviations of the
quantity itself, and 24.9\% of station-years are wrong by more than
1.0\,$^{\circ}$C. An error of that size turns a thermally stressful year into an
unremarkable one.

Current practice assumes that the amount of missing data decides whether the
derived number may be used, and two families of rule encode that assumption
(Table~\ref{tab:map}). Collection standards specify what must be present:
\citeA{mauger2015}, writing minimum standards for a state whose records feed
regional analyses, require a minimum sampling duration of one calendar month and
recommend a deployment period of 1 June to 31 August ``to capture the thermal
maxima''; the same standards govern which of those records enter the regional
assessment \cite{mauger2017}. Quantitative guidance constrains what may be absent:
\citeA{johnson2021} report that ``datasets with less than seven weeks of
consecutively missing data enabled estimation of all annual signal parameters
with reasonable accuracy'', expanding to ``approximately 20 weeks'' when missing
data are imputed. The gap-filling literature optimises the accuracy of the
filled series rather than the admissibility of the completed year
\cite{mohseni1998,li2017}, and the closest prior work fills incomplete summer
records so that a thermal peak can be computed, applies that filling uniformly,
declines no station-year, and admits July--August-only records on the
climatological position of the hottest window \cite{beaufort2022}. Large-sample
thermal models optimise daily or reach-scale prediction skill
\cite{isaak2017,corona2025,kratzert2023}, and selective-prediction methods
control error at a fixed rejection rate \cite{geifman2017,zaoui2020}. None of
these rules measures how much of the target statistic's own support a record
contains.

The assumption is plausible for some statistics and wrong for others. An annual
mean is a sum over days, so removing $k$ days removes $k$ terms and it matters
little which. A hottest 30-day mean is an extreme supported on a short window:
if the outage misses that window the statistic is recoverable almost exactly
however long the outage is, and if the outage covers it the statistic must be
inferred however short the outage is. The direction of a completeness rule's
error is therefore opposite in the two cases, and an average over a mixed archive
conceals the reversal. The literature is consistent with that reversal without
having isolated it: reported timing effects on annual thermal parameters are
inconsistent across regions, years and parameters \cite{johnson2021};
reconstruction bias depends on where the target window falls
\cite{beaufort2022}; systematic, time-varying instrument bias rather than random
noise dominates long-term monitoring error \cite{jackson2025}; and
statistic-aware completeness conventions do exist in climate monitoring, but they
are defined by counts or seasons rather than by the statistic's support
\cite{zhang2011}. Irregular sampling and sampling artifacts likewise bias means
and trends and decide which model structures remain trustworthy
\cite{grey2024}, and reconstruction frameworks have been built for regulated and
data-sparse basins, including Chinese catchments and the Yangtze mainstem
\cite{huang2023,wang2025}; those studies answer estimation and reconstruction
questions, not the archive-admission question for a named annual extreme.

Testing the assumption requires separating two properties of a gap that real
records entangle. Gap length and the share of the target window that is hidden
correlate at $-0.86$ in our own panel, so a comparison across real gaps cannot
say whether a rule works because it avoids long gaps, because it avoids badly
placed ones, or because both simply track how much record the station has. A
second obstacle is truth: in exactly the years where the admission decision
matters, the target statistic is unknown by construction, so the benefit of a
rule cannot be measured where it would be applied. A third is that evaluations
report a rule's discrimination rather than the composition of the archive it
leaves behind, and composition is what decides which catchments remain available
for analysis.

To address these limitations we assemble 5114 complete river station-years from
146 networks and nine national agencies, place 51524 outages on complete years so
that the two properties of a gap can be varied independently, and score
admission rules on held-out river networks, on sealed panels that took no part in
development, and --- without any truth --- on the 2422 records in this same
archive that really do have gaps. Three questions organise the paper, and the
last matters most, because a screening rule is judged by the archive it leaves
behind. First, which property of a gap governs the error of a derived thermal
statistic, and does the answer depend on which statistic is being computed?
Second, is that dependence a dichotomy between two regimes or a continuous
transition, where does it turn over, and how much of the gain survives the gap
calendar these networks actually keep? Third, what does conditioning on it change
for the error among retained station-years, for which stations survive in the
archive, and for the undecided records?

\section{Study system, data and identification design}

\subsection{The statistic, its support, and the share a gap hides}

Let a station-year be a sequence $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ of daily mean water
temperatures, $T\in\{365,366\}$, and let $q(\cdot)$ be the derived statistic of
interest. The family we sweep is the hottest consecutive $k$-day mean
\begin{equation}
q_k(y) \;=\; \max_{t}\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=t}^{t+k-1} y_i ,
\label{eq:qk}
\end{equation}
which is the annual maximum at $k=1$ and the annual mean at $k=T$; the coldest
30-day mean, the annual amplitude, and the sine-fit mean, phase and amplitude of
\citeA{johnson2021} are scored alongside.

A gap is a binary mask $m\in\{0,1\}^T$; the analyst observes the days where
$m_t=0$. A reconstruction $\hat y = R(y_{\bar m}, Z)$ completes the year using
side information $Z$ --- synchronous donor stations, air temperature, the
station's own history --- and reports $q(\hat y)$. The quantity that matters is
the error of the reported statistic,
\begin{equation}
L \;=\; \bigl| q(\hat y) - q(y) \bigr| ,
\end{equation}
and a station-year is called \emph{consequential} when $L>\tau$ for a tolerance
$\tau$ in the units of $q$.

The property of the gap we propose as the governing one follows from how much
each day moves the statistic. Writing $w^q_t = |\partial q(y)/\partial y_t|$ for
the discrete influence function of $q$ at $y$ \cite{hampel1974}, the
\emph{hidden mass} of a gap is the share of that influence it removes,
\begin{equation}
h_q(m) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{t:\, m_t=1} w^q_t}{\sum_t w^q_t}.
\label{eq:hidden}
\end{equation}
For the annual mean every $w_t$ is equal and $h_q$ is the missing fraction; for
the hottest $k$-day mean the influence is concentrated on the $k$ days achieving
the maximum and $h_q$ is the share of that window the gap hides; for the sine-fit
parameters the weights are smooth functions of day of year --- a constant for the
fitted mean, $|\sin(\theta_t-\phi)|$ for the amplitude, which is largest at both
extremes, and $|\cos(\theta_t-\phi)|$ for the phase, which is largest a
quarter-year from the peak. Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} therefore contains the
incumbent axis as a special case rather than opposing it, which is why the two
regimes reported below are ends of one scale.

Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} cannot be evaluated at decision time, because the
weights depend on the days the gap hides. What an analyst has is the
\emph{climatological} support: the window the station's other complete years say
the maximum should fall in. That estimate has an error, and
Section~\ref{sec:dilate} shows the error is large enough to change what a
screening rule should condition on.

\subsection{Records, and what each dataset is for}
\label{sec:records}

Daily water-temperature series were assembled from nine national providers: ARSO
(Slovenia), CHMI (Czechia), FOEN (Switzerland), GKD Bayern and LUBW (Germany),
NVE (Norway), Rijkswaterstaat (Netherlands), SYKE (Finland) and the USGS (United
States). Acquisition contracts differ, and we audited each one against the
provider's own documentation
(\texttt{measurement\_provenance\_dictionary.csv}).
Five providers document a daily mean and the parser reads it: USGS
(\texttt{parameterCd=00010}, \texttt{statCd=00003}), FOEN
(\texttt{data\_1day\_mean}), GKD Bayern (\texttt{Tageswerte}, column
\texttt{Mittelwert}), NVE HydAPI (resolution 1440, documented as mean) and, for
the transport panel below, the UK Environment Agency (measure period 86400,
``daily mean Temperature''), with LUBW's \texttt{Tagesmittelwert} documented but
its opaque workbook column only partially re-verified. Four providers do not meet
that contract as read: CHMI's parser averages 10-minute \emph{instantaneous}
values ($T_O$) even though the provider also publishes a daily-mean product
($T_D$); Rijkswaterstaat's parser averages raw \texttt{NVT} readings even though
a live 24\,h mean parameter (\texttt{GEM24H}) exists; SYKE's public day values
are morning nearshore reference readings (08:00, 20\,cm below the surface) rather
than 24\,h means; and ARSO's daily value has no published mean-versus-hour
statement. We therefore treat ``daily mean'' as a contract that holds for part of
the panel and report the consequence: restricted to the five providers whose
daily value is a documented provider daily mean, the development contrast is
$-5.7$ points ($[-6.5,-4.9]$, 84 networks) and the reserved contrast $-6.6$
($[-7.9,-5.3]$, 29 networks), against $-6.4$ and $-7.1$ for the full panel
(\texttt{amendments/provider\_contract\_sensitivity/}). The sealed confirmation is
USGS-only and so is entirely inside the documented stratum. The three SYKE
stations remain flagged in
\texttt{results/wrr\_submission\_ready/measurement\_provenance\_stations.csv}
and are read as a sensitivity stratum, not as proven daily means. A
\emph{network} is one provider's group of stations on a common river system. It
is the unit at which donor stations are available to one another, and it is the
clustering unit for every interval reported here, because station-years within a
network share weather, regulation and instrumentation.

Waterbody class was assigned per network and non-river objects --- seas, lakes,
canals, estuaries, deltas --- are excluded rather than averaged into a river
result. Reference series are never imputed: a calendar year with any missing day
supplies no truth. Stations with fewer than four fully observed calendar years
are excluded because the estimator protocol requires them. This leaves
\textbf{146 networks, 457 stations and 5114 fully observed station-years}.

The same 457 stations contribute \textbf{2422 further station-years that are
genuinely incomplete}. These carry no truth and are therefore not part of the
scored population; they are the population a screening rule exists for.
Section~\ref{sec:deploy} reports overlap-rule decisions on that panel; the frozen
certificate can also be applied through a truth-free \texttt{predict} interface
(repository amendment
\texttt{results/wrr\_submission\_ready/e2\_certificate\_predict/}).

Station coordinates were assembled for all 457 stations, from the providers' own
catalogues, from the USGS NWIS site service for 195 United States gauges, and
from ARSO's public station feed, and daily 2\,m air temperature was joined from
NASA POWER for the whole panel, 1981 to 2026 \cite{nasapower2026}. For the
United States subset, basin characteristics were joined from GAGES-II
\cite{falcone2011} on the eight-digit gauge identifier, matching \textbf{140 of
204} eligible stations across 57 networks. Basin attributes are used to ask which
rivers are hard (Section~\ref{sec:hard}) and which rivers a screen keeps
(Section~\ref{sec:repr}); they are deliberately not used inside the screening
rule, because they are unavailable for the other eight agencies.

Table~\ref{tab:quantities} lists every quantity the analysis uses, what it means
physically, where its value comes from, and whether it was calibrated. Networks
--- never station-years --- were split into development and reserved, stratified

\subsection{Gap design: two arms, and why both are needed}

A case is one station-year under one gap realization. Two arms are used and
reported separately, because they answer different questions and are not one
population. The \emph{empirical} arm samples real gap runs --- length and start
month --- from the same network's own records and transplants them onto fully
observed years, so that operational numbers reflect the geometry agencies
experience. The \emph{stratified} arm crosses gap length
$\{14,30,60,90,120,180,270\}$ days with the overlap between the gap and the
station's climatological peak window, so that the response surface is estimable
rather than accidental. The design yields \textbf{51524 cases}, 40704
development and 10820 reserved.

Both arms transplant a mask onto a year whose values were observed, which assumes
the mask is uninformative about the values it hides.
Section~\ref{sec:assume} tests that assumption against the archive rather than
asserting it. Gaps are placed inside one calendar year; failures that straddle
New Year damage two annual statistics while hiding neither warm season, and the
design cannot represent that case.

Each endpoint is scored against a tolerance equal to the same fraction, 0.47, of
\emph{its own} interannual standard deviation at the same stations. For the
annual peak that scale is 1.06\,$^{\circ}$C, giving a primary tolerance of
0.50\,$^{\circ}$C; the scale falls monotonically with support width, from
1.18\,$^{\circ}$C at $k=1$ to 0.55\,$^{\circ}$C at $k=365$. The fraction is swept
at 0.25, 0.47 and 1.0, and no conclusion about which axis wins is claimed unless
it is invariant across the sweep. The reference series carries instrument error
of roughly 0.1 to 0.2\,$^{\circ}$C, so the primary tolerance is about three times
sensor accuracy; Section~\ref{sec:assume} perturbs the reference by
0.15\,$^{\circ}$C and repeats the analysis.

\section{Methods}

Each choice below answers one of the obstacles in the introduction, and the
order of the subsections follows that logic rather than the order in which the
code runs: the reconstruction defines what a deployed rule may read; the rule
defines the two candidate conditioning axes and the information barrier between
them and the truth; the test protocol fixes the operating point, the clustered
inference unit and the falsification tests declared before scoring.

\subsection{Reconstruction, and what each estimator is allowed to see}

Every case is completed by each supported estimator: the statistic computed from
recorded days alone (current practice), a day-of-year median climatology, a
smoothed climatology, linear interpolation where both flanks exist, ridge
regression on the visible subset of synchronous donors, and an air-driven ridge
with lagged air temperature and harmonics. Estimators are fit leave-one-year-out
within the station, which is what offline archive repair permits;
Section~\ref{sec:assume} repeats the study under the stricter protocol in which
only prior years are available. Support is declared per method: donor regression
does not read a flanking observation, so requiring one would discard usable
cases, and absent donor columns are never imputed but the model is refit on the
visible subset.

One weakness in that library was worth removing before the comparison was run.
Air temperature was available for 2.3\% of development cases in an earlier
version of this analysis, so the reconstruction this field actually uses was
effectively absent, and 36 of the 110 development networks have no synchronous
donor at all. With coordinates joined for all 457 stations and NASA POWER pulled
for the whole panel, air forcing now covers 98.6\% of development cases and
100\% of reserved cases. The retrospective reserved holdout is not re-scored
against the new forcing; Section~\ref{sec:fair} reports it as a
development-panel sensitivity analysis.

The reconstruction layer is not the contribution of this paper and is kept
deliberately simple. Its role is to make the comparison between conditioning
variables fair, which requires only that the estimator paired with the incumbent
rule is the strongest deployable one; Section~\ref{sec:fair} tests that
requirement directly rather than assuming it.

\subsection{The screening rule, and the information it is forbidden to read}

For each case the screen predicts an upper bound on the absolute error each
supported estimator would produce, selects the estimator with the smallest
bound, and emits a conformal interval on the statistic together with an
admission decision at an analyst-chosen tolerance. Its inputs are the first six
rows of Table~\ref{tab:quantities}. No input may read a day the gap hides, and
that restriction is enforced in code rather than by convention.

Calibration is split conformal \cite{lei2018}, and the risk-control framing
follows the distribution-free line of \citeA{angelopoulos2022} with the caveat
that selective observation is itself an existing problem statement
\cite{bai2026}; what is specific here is that selection happens among
reconstructions chosen per station-year. Two properties matter. It is
\emph{selection-conditional}, because taking the smallest of several bounds is
a winner's curse that per-estimator calibration cannot see, and
\emph{multiplicative}, because the error scale spans an order of magnitude
between a gap that misses the peak window and one that covers it. Fitting,
calibration and test networks are always disjoint.

\subsection{Test protocol: fixed operating point, clustered unit, declared falsifications}

Before any rule was fit we measured, on development cases, how much a rule with
perfect foresight could win inside each candidate action space, and compared it
with the margin the reserved panel can resolve. Two natural framings --- adopt or
reject a fixed reconstruction, and choose among reconstruction methods --- have
ceilings of $+0.038$ and $+0.080\,^{\circ}$C against a threshold of
$0.136\,^{\circ}$C, and were not pursued. Retain-or-exclude clears its gate by a
factor of five and is the action space studied here. A decision experiment whose
ceiling is below its own resolvable margin cannot produce evidence in either
direction, and running one consumes a reserved panel for nothing.

Every interval resamples whole networks, because station-years within a network
are not independent and reusing a station-year under several gap realizations
does not create independent observations. The pre-registered primary criterion,
frozen before the reserved panel was opened, is that at the retention of the
matched length rule the screen must reduce the consequential-error rate by at
least five percentage points with a network-clustered interval excluding zero.
Precision was computed in advance: the reserved panel resolves 1.44 points, so
the declared effect is 3.5 times the resolution.

Where several endpoints are compared, the direction of each contrast was
predicted in advance from the form of the statistic, which is the defence against
multiplicity. Three of those predictions were not met, and
Sections~\ref{sec:dilate} and \ref{sec:sine} report them as failures rather than
reshaping them into successes.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The phenomenon: position, not length, decides what an extreme loses}

Whether an incomplete year can carry a summer extreme depends on where its gap
falls and hardly at all on how long the gap is. One real record shows the two
ends of the range: a 30-day gap over the peak window costs $3.99\,^{\circ}$C in
the hottest 30-day mean, the same 30 days in late autumn costs nothing, and the
annual mean responds in the opposite order. The reversal is systematic across
five annual endpoints, and it is large enough to change which station-years an
archive should keep.

\subsubsection{Computing the peak from recorded days alone costs more than a year-to-year standard deviation}

Current practice is expensive before any rule is proposed. On development cases,
computing the annual peak from recorded days alone --- the default once a
station-year passes a completeness screen --- gives a mean absolute error of
\textbf{1.305\,$^{\circ}$C, or 1.23 interannual standard deviations} of the
quantity being measured, with 24.9\% of station-years wrong by more than
1.0\,$^{\circ}$C. Substituting a day-of-year climatology over the gap gives
0.402\,$^{\circ}$C, the best supported reconstruction 0.225\,$^{\circ}$C with air
forcing available for the whole panel (0.242\,$^{\circ}$C without it), and an
oracle over the estimator set 0.130\,$^{\circ}$C. The error is therefore
dominated not by which station-years are kept but by whether the record is
repaired at all, which fixes the size of the question the rest of the paper
addresses: given that a year has been repaired, which repaired years can be
trusted.

\subsubsection{The share of the support a gap hides orders every endpoint, and the ordering reverses}

Table~\ref{tab:axes} reports, for five endpoints, how well each candidate

between extremes and averages, every clustered interval excludes zero, and the

endpoint, which the support account predicted before it was measured because a
shorter extreme is more localised in time; peak-window overlap has an AUC of

information about the winter minimum. The last row is not evidence that cold
extremes are a length-governed exception, and reading it that way would be a
mistake: the two support axes in the table are defined on the warm season. Built
on the endpoint's own season --- a climatological coldest-30-day window and the

defined on the season the statistic lives in; it does not license a
warm-season axis for a cold endpoint. That cold-endpoint analysis is exploratory:
it uses the development panel and does not touch the sealed confirmation.

\subsection{The response is conditional: where the gain holds and where it disappears}

The advantage of conditioning on support is not general. It depends on how wide
the estimand's support is, it survives only in part under the gap calendar these
networks actually keep, and it differs across agencies and across how strictly
their daily values are checked. Reporting only the pooled number would hide all
three dependencies.

\subsubsection{The transition is continuous, and a fixed narrow window turns over near a three-month support}

Table~\ref{tab:axes} evaluates the question at two points and reads the gap
between them as a dichotomy. Sweeping the hottest-$k$-day mean of
Equation~\eqref{eq:qk} from $k=1$ to $k=365$ evaluates it everywhere
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, Figure~\ref{fig:law}a). The advantage of a fixed 30-day
peak window --- the axis the screen actually uses --- is flat at about $+0.075$
AUC while the estimand's support is a month or less, falls to $+0.050$ at two
months and to $+0.002$ at three, and is negative beyond that. The 90-day grid
point is statistically indistinguishable from zero (95\% interval $-0.018$ to
$+0.026$, positive in 55\% of network-clustered replicates), and the first
clearly negative grid point is 182 days, so the crossing is only located between
60 and 182 days: a bootstrap of the whole curve puts its 95\% interval at
$[77,107]$ days and it moves between 85 and 102 days across the tolerance grid,
while the opened reserved panel's interval ($[104,136]$) overlaps it rather than
displacing it. We therefore report a range and not a day count. The same sweep
supports a second, different statement: a conditioning window dilated around the
estimand's own support keeps its advantage over whole-year completeness out to
about 359 days (95\% interval 351 to 364, and it fails to cross at all in 7\% of
replicates), ending only at the trivial $k=365$ coincidence, and the advantage is
a hump that peaks near 60 days rather than decaying monotonically. The mid-range
crossing is thus a property of holding the conditioning window narrow and fixed,
not of the support idea itself. Nor is one window enough across the continuum:
a single 60-to-90-day window is within 0.0004 AUC of each endpoint's own optimum
up to a 90-day support, but costs 0.083 AUC at the annual-mean end. These continuum numbers are exploratory
analyses developed after the reserved panel was opened; they motivate the
screening axis but are not the sealed USGS confirmation reported with the data
description. The two regimes of Table~\ref{tab:axes} are useful ends of that
exploratory scale, not a proven universal dichotomy.

\subsubsection{On the estimands the standing guidance was derived for, the guidance is right}

The sine-fit parameters also connect to a physical reading that is worth keeping
separate from the statistical one: air--water phase and amplitude carry
information about groundwater and riparian influence \cite{hare2023}, and a
support-conditioned screen improves the error of those parameters without
claiming to identify the process behind them.

\label{sec:sine}

\citeA{johnson2021} fit a sine wave by linear regression and take a mean, a phase
and an amplitude; they do not compute a hottest 30-day mean. Reproducing that
estimator on our panel puts the two accounts on the same footing for the first
time (Table~\ref{tab:sine}, Figure~\ref{fig:law}c). A completeness threshold is
the correct axis for the fitted mean, at 0.845 against 0.672 for peak-window
overlap, and is within 0.02 AUC of the best axis for the phase. The ordering is
the same under the authors' own protocol, in which the sine is fitted to
whatever days are present with no repair at all: peak overlap 0.667, 0.743 and
0.590 against whole-year missingness 0.902, 0.842 and 0.898 for the mean,
amplitude and phase, at base rates of 32\% to 34\% consequential against 10\% to
12\% after repair.

Two reporting rules come out of this endpoint. Phase is only identifiable when
the fitted amplitude stands above the noise: the standard error of a
three-parameter sine fit is $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}/A$ radians, so requiring a 95\%
interval no wider than the phase tolerance used here gives $A/\sigma \ge 4.5$,
which retains 70.7\% of cases and leaves the comparison above unchanged
($+0.0126$ against $+0.0127$ on all cases); under the authors' own unrepaired
protocol the defect is active rather than latent, with 0.10\% of cases below
$A/\sigma=1$ carrying a median phase error of 60 days against 91 days for a
random phase. The rule is an identifiability floor, not a quality guarantee: the
error rises again above $A/\sigma\approx8$. Second, the fitted mean and phase
are the regime the standing guidance was derived for, and on them it is right.

The third declared prediction failed here. We expected that for the phase, whose
influence is largest a quarter-year from the peak, two 61-day windows around the
spring and autumn limbs would beat both peak overlap and gap length. They beat
peak overlap decisively ($+0.249$ AUC, 95\% CI 0.231 to 0.270) and they do not
beat gap length (0.831 against 0.836). Weighting every day by the influence
function of Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} instead recovers the advantage: 0.847,
which beats the whole-year missing fraction by $+0.013$ (0.009, 0.017) and peak
overlap by $+0.265$ (0.249, 0.285).

\subsubsection{The gain also depends on the agency and on how strictly its values are checked}

Pooled numbers conceal two further dependencies. By agency, the sealed transport
panel gives $-4.3$ points on 16 Swedish networks ($[-6.3,-3.2]$), $-4.9$ on 14
French networks ($[-8.5,-1.5]$) and $-6.4$ on the two English networks
($[-8.0,+1.3]$, an interval that a two-network sample cannot narrow); the
Norwegian secondary stratum gives $-6.1$ ($[-8.7,-4.6]$). By assurance, the
globally checked stratum gives $-6.2$ points ($[-8.4,-4.5]$) against $-4.6$
($[-6.2,-3.3]$) for providers that publish their values unqualified, so part of
the pooled gain is carried by records whose quality flags are stronger. The same
caution applies to the measurement contract itself: five providers document the
daily mean that the parser reads, two publish sub-daily values that the project
averages, and four do not document a daily mean at all
(\texttt{measurement\_provenance\_dictionary.csv}). Restricting the development
panel to the documented-daily-mean stratum gives a contrast of $-5.7$ points
($[-6.5,-4.9]$, 84 networks) rather than $-6.4$, and the reserved panel $-6.6$
($[-7.9,-5.3]$, 29 networks) rather than $-7.1$
(\texttt{amendments/provider\_contract\_sensitivity/}). The direction and the
significance of the effect are unchanged in every stratum; its size is not a
single number.

\subsection{The mechanism: hidden support and donor information control the error}

Two measurable quantities organise the pattern: the share of the estimand's own
support that a gap hides, which enters the error through the influence function
of the statistic, and the synchronous anomaly information the surrounding
stations carry. Both are visible at decision time, and neither is a restatement
of gap length.

\subsubsection{A support must be located before it can be conditioned on, so the useful window is wider than the statistic}
\label{sec:dilate}

Two predictions declared for this round were not met, and both failures have one
cause. We predicted that the axis restricted to the estimand's own support would
be at least as good as any hand-chosen axis at every $k$, and that its advantage
would decay monotonically. Neither holds: the advantage is a hump peaking at a
60-day support, and for supports of 15 days or less the fixed 30-day peak window
beats the estimand's own support, by 0.064 AUC at $k=1$.

Table~\ref{tab:dilate} gives the reason. The gain from conditioning on a window
wider than the statistic tracks the year-to-year wander of the realised window's
location almost exactly: it is $+0.102$ AUC at $k=1$, where the climatological
window is the worst estimate of where this year's window will fall and the
wander is 16.7 days, and vanishes at $k\ge 90$, where the wander has fallen to
7.4 days and the support is wide enough to contain its own location error. A
screening rule therefore does not condition on the support; it conditions on the
support \emph{dilated by the uncertainty in the support's location}, and a
dilation of roughly two location standard deviations on each side is what the
data choose.

One consequence is practical. AUC at the best conditioning window is between 0.85
and 0.86 for every estimand from the annual maximum to the half-year mean, so an

metric. A second is uncomfortable: the screen deployed below conditions on a
30-day window and is therefore leaving 0.025 AUC on the table for its own
endpoint.

\subsubsection{Error grows along hidden support rather than along length, and donors supply anomaly information}

The mechanism behind that scale is measurable rather than only plausible.
Binning development cases on both properties simultaneously and measuring the
growth of error along each with the other averaged out, the hottest 30-day mean
grows $3.5\times$ along the missing-days axis and $9.6\times$ along the
peak-overlap axis, while the annual mean grows $10.2\times$ and $1.5\times$
respectively: the two ratios swap by roughly an order of magnitude in each
direction. For the peak, once the support is in the model, gap length adds
nothing measurable --- its permutation importance is $-0.004$ against 0.194 for
peak overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}c) --- and nested feature sets grouped by
network rise from AUC 0.742 with length alone to 0.845 adding the support, 0.858
adding the observed-peak margin and 0.884 adding donor quality
(Figure~\ref{fig:mech}b).

Two qualifications belong with that. Length is not irrelevant: holding peak
overlap at zero, the consequential-error rate still rises from 0.6\% at 1 to 30
days to 8.7\% at 121 to 200 days. And in a linear score that already contains
warm-season coverage the length term takes a small negative weight, which is
collinearity --- the two correlate at $-0.86$ --- and not evidence that longer
gaps are safer. That correlation is also why a timing-versus-length labelling is
wrong: warm-season coverage is a count of missing days restricted to a window,
which is a length measure with a domain restriction, and it is the domain
restriction and not the timing that does the work.

Donor information enters the residual through quality rather than quantity.
Halving the donor set by training-period correlation costs $+0.084\,^{\circ}$C
(95\% interval 0.064 to 0.110) when the comparison is re-run with the donor count

(34\% of cases). Replacing donor anomalies with phase-shuffled surrogates while
preserving each donor's seasonal cycle costs $+0.234\,^{\circ}$C (0.149 to 0.299),
so donors supply synchronous anomaly information and not merely a seasonal shape.

\subsubsection{The calibrated bound is honest enough to interpret}

Everything that follows uses a predicted error bound to rank station-years, so
the bound is checked before it is used. On the reserved networks the nominal 90\%
interval covers 90.9\% of cases marginally, inside the pre-declared band of 0.87
to 0.93, and 86.3\% to 94.9\% within every pre-declared stratum
(Figure~\ref{fig:calib}a). Median widths adapt with the quantity they are meant
to track, from 0.06\,$^{\circ}$C when the peak window is untouched to
1.87\,$^{\circ}$C when most of it is hidden.

Those strata pool across networks, and conformal validity assumes an
exchangeability that fails between river networks. Coverage inside individual
reserved networks has mean 0.904 and median 0.913 but a minimum of 0.778, with
five of 36 networks below 0.85. The pooled interval is an honest statement about
a population of networks and is \emph{not} a guarantee at a single new site;
calibrating on the per-network conformal quantiles at the 0.9 level puts no
network below 0.85 for a 1.5-fold widening. The screening results below are identical under every
calibration to four decimal places, because the rank order the screen uses is a
monotone transform of the bound, so a reader who distrusts the intervals need not
distrust the frontier.

\subsubsection{The incumbent is not handicapped, including by the absence of the field’s own reconstruction}
\label{sec:fair}

A screening comparison can be rigged by pairing the incumbent with a weak
reconstruction, so the incumbent is paired here with every reconstruction in the
study, including one nobody can deploy (Table~\ref{tab:fair}).

The screen beats the incumbent armed with a reconstruction nobody can run, and
the reconstruction paired with the incumbent throughout is the strongest of the
deployable options rather than the weakest. Air-driven reconstruction is reported
twice, because the earlier label was wrong: air-temperature regression remains a
workhorse for data-sparse basins \cite{sanikhani2026,you2026}, and the library's air ridge with a fixed
penalty ($\alpha=1.0$, seven lags, whole-year fit, penalised) was described as
``in the manner of \citeA{beaufort2022}'' but shares only its input with that
paper, and is now labelled as what it is. Reimplementing the cited method
faithfully --- per-station OLS of summer water temperature on a right-aligned
moving average of air temperature, 2--10 day windows chosen per station by

15.16\% (paired difference $-2.40$ points, $[-3.20,-1.62]$, 1200 network-clustered

(\texttt{amendments/faithful\_baseline/}). The axis conclusion does not depend on
the estimator library: the timing-minus-length AUC contrast is $+0.070$ with the
old forcing and $+0.081$ with the new. The screen uses the new information,
selecting the air-driven estimator for 20.0\% of cases against 0.07\% before, and
donor regression for 53.1\%.

\subsubsection{On records that really have gaps, the two rules disagree on one year in six}
\label{sec:deploy}

Every number above is computed on a complete station-year with a synthetic hole
punched in it, which is the only way to have a truth. The same archive contains
2422 genuinely incomplete station-years, a third of the total, which carry no
truth and have never been decided. Three things can be asked of them without
inventing one (Figure~\ref{fig:deploy}).

They are not a marginal population. The median incomplete year is missing 9 days
and the 90th percentile 128; 28.7\% of them have at least one day of the
station's climatological peak window missing, and 261 have more than half of it
missing.

The two rules disagree on a sixth of them. At the seven-week unfilled limit the
length rule admits 83.2\% and a peak-window rule at 10\% hidden admits 81.2\% ---
near-equal retention, which is the comparison this paper insists on --- and they
\textbf{disagree on 15.1\%} of station-years: 207 are admitted by the incumbent
and rejected on the support, and 158 are rejected by the incumbent and admitted
on it. At the twenty-week imputed limit the incumbent admits 91.7\% and the
disagreement is 11.6\%, of which 267 are in the dangerous direction and 13 in the
wasteful one. The disagreement is present in every provider with more than 20
incomplete station-years, from 11.1\% to 25.7\%.

\subsubsection{Conditioning on difficulty removes mountain catchments unless the threshold is applied within strata}
\label{sec:repr}

A rule that lowers the average error by discarding hard station-years has bought
accuracy with representativeness, and in this application the hard sites are not
a random subset: Section~\ref{sec:hard} showed they are high-elevation, steep,
donor-poor catchments, which are the cold-water headwaters the summer endpoint
exists to describe.

At 70\% retention the calibrated screen's per-station retention correlates with
site elevation at Spearman $\rho=-0.26$ ($p=0.011$) on development and $-0.27$
($p=0.077$) on the reserved panel, while the incumbent length rule is
elevation-neutral at $\rho=-0.05$ ($p=0.60$) and $-0.10$ ($p=0.52$). On the
reserved panel the screen retains 77.7\% of station-years in the lowest elevation
tertile and 66.9\% in the highest, a spread of 10.8 points against 1.7 for the
length rule (Figure~\ref{fig:repr}b). By donor regime the same pattern appears on
development cases: 57.1\% at donor-less stations against 72.5\% at donor-rich
ones, a 15.4-point spread, while the length rule tilts the other way.

The distortion is cheap to remove (Table~\ref{tab:repr}). Applying the screen's
threshold within stratum rather than globally costs 0.31 points of
consequential-error rate on development cases and 0.30 on the reserved panel and
flattens both distortions. Stratifying on donor regime alone does not fix the
elevation tilt and stratifying on elevation alone does not fix the donor tilt, so
an agency that cares about both must declare both.

\subsubsection{Where it is hard: catchment attributes explain a modest share of the error}
\label{sec:hard}

The account so far is a property of estimators and estimands and would hold for
any time series. For the 140 United States stations matched to GAGES-II across 57
networks, we regressed donor synchrony and completed-year error on basin
attributes with the expected direction of each effect declared before the
regressions were run (Supporting Information Figure~S7). Site elevation and basin slope
both reduce donor synchrony (Spearman $\rho=-0.29$, 95\% CI $-0.46$ to $-0.08$,
and $\rho=-0.31$, $-0.51$ to $-0.07$), and site elevation raises both
completed-year error ($\rho=+0.24$, 0.06 to 0.43) and the consequential-error
rate ($\rho=+0.25$, 0.08 to 0.43).

The groundwater prediction taken from the literature is not reproduced, and the
reason is instructive. \citeA{johnson2021} report that filling can harm
groundwater-influenced sites, which predicts larger completed-year error at a
high baseflow index. Baseflow index does reduce donor synchrony in the predicted
direction, from 0.70 in the lowest tertile to 0.59 in the highest, although the
rank correlation interval includes zero ($\rho=-0.19$, $-0.42$ to 0.10). But the
error moves the other way: splitting at the median baseflow index, high-baseflow
stations are $-0.043\,^{\circ}$C easier than low-baseflow stations (95\% CI
$-0.081$ to $-0.004$). Across baseflow
tertiles the mean annual peak falls from 22.7\,$^{\circ}$C to 18.1\,$^{\circ}$C
($\rho=-0.47$, $-0.60$ to $-0.26$) and its interannual standard deviation from
0.98 to 0.76\,$^{\circ}$C (Supporting Information Figure~S7c), so two effects act in
opposite directions and the second is larger.

Regulation does not make station-years harder for this endpoint: reference and
non-reference basins differ by $+0.004\,^{\circ}$C (95\% CI $-0.051$ to
$+0.093$). Catchment attributes explain a modest share of the variation, a
standardised linear model reaching $R^2=0.22$ for donor synchrony and 0.13 for
completed-year error, and no attribute predicts how much a screen gains: every
correlation between a basin attribute and the per-station screening benefit has
an interval containing zero. Whether an attribute predicts what a screen
\emph{keeps} is a different question, and Section~\ref{sec:repr} answers it
differently.


\subsubsection{The conclusion survives instrument error, a stricter fitting protocol, and transfer across agencies}
\label{sec:assume}

\textbf{Missingness is weakly informative, and the effect is bounded well below
the tolerance.} Every case transplants a mask onto a year whose values were
observed, which is valid only if the mask is ignorable in the sense of
\citeA{rubin1976}; loggers are lost to floods, buried by ice and fouled in low
flow, and each of those coincides with anomalous conditions. Across the 6472
real gaps, the mean anomaly of the seven days preceding a gap, relative to the
station's own day-of-year climatology, is $+0.018\,^{\circ}$C for gaps of a week
or less ($p=0.44$), $+0.038$ for 8 to 29 days ($p=0.56$), $-0.002$ for 30 to 89
days ($p=0.98$) and $-0.215$ for 90 days and longer ($p=0.081$). Donors recording
through a gap run $+0.063\,^{\circ}$C warm during short gaps, detectable at this
sample size ($p=0.017$) and 3\% of the within-station anomaly scale. The design
is therefore defended on the magnitude of the effect rather than on its absence,
and the class that warrants a limitation is gaps of three months and longer. A
pattern-mixture check turns that defence into a bound: shifting every filled day
by $\delta\,^{\circ}$C moves the completed year's peak by $\delta$ times the
share of the realised peak window the gap hid, and across
$\delta \in \{-1,-0.5,-0.25,0,+0.25,+0.5,+1\}\,^{\circ}$C the screen keeps its
advantage at equal retention with a network-clustered interval excluding zero in
all three populations (development, the retrospective reserved panel, and the
sealed USGS panel); no bias inside that grid erases it
(\texttt{amendments/mnar\_pattern\_mixture/}). The assumption is bounded rather
than verified, which is the strongest statement these records support.

\textbf{Three further checks move the numbers and not the conclusion.}
Instrument error in these archives is systematic and time-varying as well as
random \cite{jackson2025}, which is why the check below is repeated under
correlated, drifting and step-like error and not only under white noise.
Perturbing the reference series by 0.15\,$^{\circ}$C of normal noise moves the
development contrast from 5.98 to 6.03 points, the reserved contrast from 6.21 to
6.22 points, and the mean absolute error from 0.2361 to 0.2363\,$^{\circ}$C; the
paired change is $+0.049$ points ($-0.036$, $+0.150$) on development and $+0.013$
($-0.175$, $+0.185$) on the reserved panel, so the check no longer reports a
reduction in the advantage (an earlier version evaluated the two arms with two
different reconstruction choices). Correlated error displaces the 30-day endpoint
harder at the same daily scale and still leaves the contrast within its interval:
an AR(1) process with $\rho=0.7$ by 2.25 times and a mid-year 0.3\,$^{\circ}$C
step by 2.12 times, giving 5.95 and 6.21 points (development) and 6.19 and 6.43
(reserved). A constant annual bias of $\pm0.5\,^{\circ}$C is an exact endpoint
shift, so it moves the contrast by at most 1.0 point (largest change interval
0.35 to 1.82 points, on the reserved panel at $-0.5\,^{\circ}$C) while raising the
mean absolute error to 0.30\,$^{\circ}$C. Restricting estimators to years before the gap
raises reconstruction error from 0.242 to 0.270\,$^{\circ}$C on a panel that
falls to 33633 development cases, and moves the contrast from $-6.35$ ($-7.12$,
$-5.52$) to $-5.92$ points ($-6.72$, $-4.99$) with coverage 0.906. Under
leave-one-provider-out the contrast is negative with an interval excluding zero
for all nine agencies, from $-11.6$ points (Finland) to $-1.2$ (Switzerland),
with conformal coverage between 0.82 and 0.96.

\section{Discussion}

Using 5114 complete river station-years, 51524 designed outages and two sealed
panels, we have shown that the error of an annual thermal extreme is governed by
where a gap falls rather than by how long it is, that an annual mean reverses
that ordering, and that screening on the hidden share of the statistic's support
leaves a materially better archive than a completeness threshold at equal
retention. The result agrees with the standing guidance on the estimands that
guidance was derived for, and disagrees with its transfer to extremes; the
disagreement is one of conditioning axis, not of arithmetic. Two mechanisms
account for it, one of which the data can measure and one of which they cannot.

\subsection{Two mechanisms explain the reversal, and one of them is not in the data}

The transition measured in Table~\ref{tab:sweep} is not a property of rivers, of
the estimators, or of the screening rule. It is a property of the functional
being computed, and it follows from where that functional puts its weight. A
statistic whose influence is concentrated on a few days is destroyed by losing
those days and is almost unaffected by losing any others; a statistic whose
influence is spread evenly over the year tends to degrade with how many days go
missing and is less sensitive to which. Between those two limits the influence
is neither concentrated nor uniform, and the discriminating power of a narrow
conditioning window falls as the weight it captures falls. On this panel's
exploratory width sweep, a fixed 30-day window stops beating a whole-year count
near a three-month support; that numerical crossing is panel- and climate-dependent,
not a universal constant.

Two measurements distinguish that account from a re-description of the data.
First, the gradient ratios swap by roughly an order of magnitude in each
direction between the peak and the annual mean, which is a direct measurement of
how error grows along each axis rather than an inference from discrimination.
Second, and more decisively, the account makes a prediction about a case it was
not built on. The sine-fit phase has an influence function with two broad lobes a
quarter-year from the peak, so the account predicts that peak-window overlap
should be a poor axis for it --- and it is, at 0.583 against 0.836 for gap length.
A description fitted to the extreme-versus-average contrast would not have
predicted that.

The same account explains where it failed. We predicted that conditioning on the
estimand's own support would always be best, and it is not, because the support
has to be estimated and the estimate wanders. Once the wander is measured the
failure becomes a quantitative statement rather than an exception: the gain from
widening the conditioning window tracks the wander from 16.7 days at $k=1$ to 7.4
days at $k=90$ and disappears when the support becomes wide enough to contain it.
This is the part of the result we would expect to transfer to other variables and
other climates, because nothing in it is specific to water temperature: any
estimator conditioned on an estimated support inherits the uncertainty of that
support.

\subsection{Where this agrees with the standing guidance, and where the disagreement lies}

An earlier framing of this work held that the incumbent rule gets an ordering
backwards. Section~\ref{sec:sine} shows that reading is wrong on the guidance's
own terms. \citeA{johnson2021} derive their limits for sine-fit mean, phase and
amplitude, and for those three estimands a completeness threshold is the correct
axis or within 0.02 AUC of it. What we correct is not the threshold but the habit
of carrying it across to an estimand it was not derived for.

That reading also resolves the anomaly the guidance itself reported. Its authors
observed that the timing of consecutive missing data had ``inconsistent effects
... among regions, years, and individual parameters'' on annual thermal-signal
estimates. Their three estimands have three different
influence functions --- a constant, two lobes at the extremes, two lobes at the
transitions --- and only one of the three has weight concentrated narrowly enough
for a timing effect to appear. Observing all three at once, inconsistency is the
only thing one could see. We do not contradict the observation; we decompose it,
and we can now say in advance which of their parameters should show a timing
effect and which should not.

A smaller result points the same way and was not expected. The fixed
information-conditioned policy gets slightly \emph{worse} at donor-less stations
once air forcing becomes available, because its preference order was written when
air was rare and it now prefers an air regression to a climatology at sites where
the climatology is better. A fixed ladder over estimators is not robust to a
change in what is available; a per-case selection is. That is an argument for the
method of this paper rather than against it, and we report it because it was the
opposite of what we expected.

The disagreement with the gap-filling literature is of a different kind and is
not resolved by our result. \citeA{beaufort2022} fill every record alike, and on
our panel pairing the incumbent rule with that treatment makes it worse rather
than better (Table~\ref{tab:fair}). That is not evidence against
air-temperature regression, which is the best available estimator at many of our
stations and which the screen selects for 20.0\% of cases. It is evidence that a
reconstruction chosen once for a whole archive and a reconstruction chosen per
station-year are different objects, and that the quantity a screening comparison
must hold fixed is the second.

\subsection{What changes in the judgement an agency actually makes}
\label{sec:judgement}

The archive-level aggregate is the wrong place to look for the value of a
screening rule, because it averages over the station-years where the call is
easy. A criterion-exceedance judgement is the right place, because it is made
one station-year at a time and it is where a wrong peak becomes a wrong
decision. On reserved networks the misclassification rate of a
20\,$^{\circ}$C exceedance is 6.86\% when the statistic is computed on recorded
days, 1.97\% when every year is reconstructed and not screened, 1.53\% under
length screening and 0.36\% under the screen. Restricted to the years where the
call is close the four figures are 16.84\%, 9.50\%, 7.26\% and 1.92\% within
1.0\,$^{\circ}$C of the threshold and 19.10\%, 13.12\%, 10.24\% and 3.38\%
within 0.5\,$^{\circ}$C, so the advantage is proportionally larger exactly where
it is needed. The bias in the count of years
above 20\,$^{\circ}$C falls from $-0.76$ years per station under recorded days
and $-0.32$ under length screening to $+0.06$.

That result is not an artefact of the threshold chosen. Swept from 14 to
24\,$^{\circ}$C the screen's misclassification stays between 0.18\% and 0.37\%
while the length rule stays between 0.84\% and 1.53\%. The sweep matters because
no single number is the criterion: regulatory practice uses a seven-day average
of daily maxima with criteria differing by several degrees between jurisdictions
and designated uses, 16\,$^{\circ}$C for core cold-water salmonid habitat and
13\,$^{\circ}$C for salmonid spawning \cite{wac2024}, which is why the seven-day
endpoint is reported throughout and why the threshold is swept rather than
asserted. The illustration is not a compliance test, and we can now say by how
much it misses one: on the 1591 station-years of the sealed USGS panel that
publish both statistics, the regulatory seven-day average of daily maxima sits a
median $+1.57\,^{\circ}$C above our seven-day average of daily means (5th to
95th percentile $+0.49$ to $+4.43$), and at criteria of 16, 18 and 20\,$^{\circ}$C
the two endpoints disagree on 5.5\%, 4.3\% and 10.4\% of station-years --- every
disagreement a missed exceedance and none a false one
(\texttt{amendments/regulatory\_daily\_max/}). A threshold shift matches counts
but not decisions, because the offset is nearly constant (slope $-0.002$ per
$^{\circ}$C). An agency applying a daily-maximum criterion must compute it from
daily maxima, which the archive publishes for 90 of the 91 sealed stations.

For a judgement of this kind, screening is not the best use of the calibrated
object. Discarding a station-year is a blunt response to uncertainty about a
single call: screening at the 0.5\,$^{\circ}$C tolerance judges 67.5\% of years
at 0.36\% misclassification, whereas abstaining only where the interval straddles
the threshold judges 92.7\% at 0.42\%, and doing both judges 65.7\% at 0.08\%.
Keeping 93\% of station-years rather than 68\% at essentially the same error rate
matters to anyone assembling a regional archive. The two actions answer different
questions --- screening decides what enters an archive, abstention decides what
one judgement may say --- and the same calibrated object serves both, which is an
argument for calibrating rather than for screening.

\subsection{What an agency should change, and in what order}

The first recommendation is to treat screening as estimand-specific. An agency
assembling warm-season extremes from maximum gap length should trial conditioning
on how much of a station's climatological warm window the gap hides, and should
keep length-based rules for annual means and the sine-fit regime parameters for
which standing guidance was derived. Cold extremes are not an exception to this:
the support axis has to be defined on the cold season, and when it is, it beats
the length rule for the coldest 30-day mean on the development panel; a
warm-season support axis is the wrong tool there, which is what the reversal in
Table~\ref{tab:axes} shows. On the retrospective reserved
holdout, substituting a support-aware screen takes the consequential-error rate
from 9.7\% to 2.5\% at equal retention on pooled designed outages; the
empirical-arm gain is not resolved, so this is not yet a claim about the
networks' natural failure calendar.

Maintenance-calendar pricing that previously appeared to show autumn servicing
gains of several percentage points is withdrawn from recommendations until the
cross-year outage placement is recomputed: the earlier counterfactual truncated
late-year gaps at day 366 while still labelling them by their nominal length.
Until that recompute is confirmed on networks outside the development programme,
schedule changes remain a hypothesis suggested by autumn-heavy long outages, not a
verified error-rate benefit. It cannot be replaced by an observed counterfactual
on this archive, and we checked rather than assumed that: for the 200 USGS
stations of the incomplete-year population we classified all 2921 real interior
gaps and 68180 missing days against the provider's own daily-value qualifiers,
and no cause-naming code --- maintenance, equipment, ice, seasonal or
discontinued --- occurs once. What the qualifiers do support is an archival
decomposition: 94.9\% of missing days are days the provider published no record
for at all (interval 88.7 to 98.2), 4.8\% were published only under a different
statistic of the same parameter, and 0.3\% were published for the target
statistic and dropped by the archive
(\texttt{amendments/outage\_causes/}). A rule that excludes planned-maintenance
gaps and keeps equipment failures is therefore a no-op on 0 of 1361 station-years,
while re-ingestion would recover five station-years (all 2024--2025) whose target
values the live service now serves. Redundancy investments similarly need cost and
failure-mode evidence beyond synchrony correlations on the opened panel.

Thresholds should be applied within strata when difficulty correlates with
elevation or donor poverty, or the archive can quietly lose mountain stations.
Nothing here applies to ungauged sites, because locating support uses a
station's own history. A pooled conformal interval is a statement about a
population of networks, not an automatic guarantee at a single new site;
cluster-aware calibration must be chosen on calibration data only and validated
on networks outside the development programme before it is sold as site-level
coverage.

\subsection{What generalises, and what does not}

Three parts of the result are portable. The governing quantity of
Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} is defined wherever a derived statistic is computed
from an incomplete record, and its ingredients --- an influence function and a
mask --- are available in any such setting. The protocol that measures the
transition is portable in the same way: compute the statistic over a family of
support widths, measure how far the support's location moves between periods,
dilate by that amount, and condition on the result. And the finding that a
screening rule must be specified together with its strata is a statement about
selection, not about temperature.

Three parts are not. The numerical crossover near three months depends on how
concentrated a river's thermal year is and on how far its peak wanders between
years; both are properties of this climate and this variable. The operational
gain is regime-dependent: the sealed USGS confirmation recovers a pooled
contrast of $-7.9$ points but the empirical arm only $-1.7$, and the
multi-agency transport panel, while negative as a whole ($-4.6$, $[-6.5,-3.3]$),
carries an empirical arm of $-1.9$ ($[-3.3,-1.0]$) --- real, but far smaller than
the designed-outage figure an agency might quote. Catchment associations rest on 140 of
204 stations in one country and should be read as attribute correlations on that
subset, not as a geology-versus-terrain law.

One boundary deserves naming separately, because it limits interpretation rather
than transfer. The mechanism argument is supported by an influence-function
derivation, by measured error gradients, by a continuum with a located crossover
and by an out-of-sample prediction about the phase. It is not supported by an
independent observation of the internal quantity itself, because the hidden mass
of Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} is by construction unobservable on the records it
matters for. A different representation that reproduced the same gradients would
not be excluded by our evidence.

\subsection{What the remaining uncertainty changes, and what it does not}

Uncertainty enters this study in four places, and it is worth separating what
each one moves. Measurement error in the reference series changes the numbers in
the third decimal and changes neither the mechanism nor the recommendation:
0.15\,$^{\circ}$C of noise moves the reserved contrast from 6.21 to 6.22 points,
and time-correlated error displaces the endpoint twice as hard while still
leaving the contrast inside its interval.
Fitting protocol changes the numbers by about half a point and changes nothing
else: restricting estimators to prior years moves the contrast from $-6.35$ to
$-5.92$. Neither of these is capable of overturning a conclusion.

The other two are. The choice of population changes the practical conclusion
completely: on the arm built from the gap geometry these networks actually
experience the effect is $-0.71$ points with an interval containing zero, and any
reader who needs a number for their own archive needs the arm that matches their
gap regime rather than the pooled figure. And the absence of an independent
observation of the internal quantity limits the mechanism judgement, as the
previous section states: we can show that conditioning on an estimate of the
support discriminates better, and we cannot show that no other representation
would do as well.

Two further limits belong here because they are specific rather than generic.
Missingness is weakly informative and the transplant design is defended on the
magnitude of that effect rather than on its absence, which makes the defence
weakest for gaps of 90 days and longer, where the leading flank runs
$0.215\,^{\circ}$C cold. And the archive-level consequence is small: the choice
of rule moves a regional mean annual peak by less than 0.1\,$^{\circ}$C. We
report that because it deflates a claim we might have made. At the level of a
regional aggregate the decision that matters is whether to repair; the rule
matters per station-year, and it matters for who is in the archive.

\section{Conclusions}

Using 5114 complete station-years from 146 river networks, 51524 designed
outages, and sealed panels scored once outside the development programme, we
quantified what an incomplete year can still support. Three answers follow.

First, the governing property of a gap is its position, not its length: the
hottest 30-day mean grows $9.6\times$ along the hidden-support axis and
$3.5\times$ along the length axis, the annual mean reverses the two, and a frozen
screen conditioned on hidden support cuts consequential errors for the summer
peak on the retrospective holdout from 9.7\% to 2.5\% at 70\% retention
($-7.1$ points, $[-9.0,-5.4]$), with
$-7.9$ ($[-10.2,-5.8]$) on 39 sealed USGS networks and $-4.6$ ($[-6.5,-3.3]$) on
a pre-registered multi-agency panel of 32 networks.

Second, the gain is conditional in three ways that a pooled number hides: it
depends on the support width of the estimand, it collapses towards $-0.7$ to
$-1.9$ points under the gap calendar these networks actually keep, and it is
smaller ($-4.6$ against $-6.2$ points) where providers publish unchecked values.
Completeness thresholds remain the correct axis for annual means and the
sine-fit parameters, which is where the standing guidance was derived.

Third, the practical consequence is an estimand-specific admission decision
rather than a new universal threshold. An agency assembling warm-season extremes
should screen on how much of the climatological peak window is hidden and should
apply the threshold within strata, because difficulty-based screening otherwise
thins high, steep and donor-poor catchments. Autumn maintenance benefits and
``annual peak never observed'' counts are not retained as verified effects: the
former needs an outage-cause record this archive does not publish, and the latter
currently counts wholly masked \emph{climatological} windows, whose positive
predictive value for a wholly unobserved realised peak is 0.74.
