\begin{abstract}
Thermal archives are screened before use, almost always by a completeness
threshold: a minimum share of the year, or a maximum consecutive gap. Both
measure the size of an outage and are applied to whichever statistic an analyst
wants. We show that the governing quantity is the share of the target
statistic's own support that the outage hides, and that the familiar contrast
between outage timing and outage length is the two ends of one continuous
scale. On 5114 fully observed river station-years from 146 networks and nine
agencies, with 51524 outage realizations, we sweep the support of the estimand
from one day to a full year: the advantage of a peak-window axis over a
completeness axis decays from 0.074 AUC and crosses zero at a support of 91
days. Because a support must be located before it can be conditioned on, and
its location wanders between years by 7 to 17 days, the best conditioning set is
that support dilated by the wander; one window of 60 to 90 days serves every
warm-season endpoint. A pre-registered screen scored once on 36 reserved
networks cuts the consequential-error rate from 9.7\% to 2.5\% at equal
retention. Applied to 2422 real incomplete station-years it reverses 15.1\% of
admissions and removes the 17 years published guidance admits with the annual
peak never observed. Two boundaries are equally prominent: the gain is not
resolvable under the outage geometry these networks experience, and a screen
selecting on difficulty selects against high-elevation catchments unless
stratified.

\begin{plainlanguagesummary}
Rivers are getting warmer, and the question managers ask is how hot a river got
last summer and whether that is changing. The answer comes from a year of daily
temperature readings, but those records have holes in them: loggers fail, get
buried by ice, or are simply not visited. Before a year of data is used,
somebody has to decide whether to trust it. Today that decision is made by
counting how much data is missing. We show that counting is the wrong question.
What matters is whether the missing days are the ones the number you want
actually depends on. An annual average depends on every day a little; a summer
peak depends almost entirely on one hot stretch. So a short outage over that
stretch is worse than a long one that misses it, while for an annual average the
opposite holds. We measure exactly where the switch happens, give agencies a
rule they can apply in a spreadsheet, and test it on 2422 real incomplete
records. We also find that when gauges fail matters as much as which records get
discarded: servicing loggers in autumn rather than spring protects the summer
peak.
\end{plainlanguagesummary}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The quantity this paper is about is the error of a derived thermal statistic,
not the error of a daily temperature series. Cold-water habitat assessment,
thermal-regime classification and criterion-exceedance accounting all reduce a
year of daily water temperature to a single number: a hottest 30-day mean, an
annual mean, a seasonal amplitude \cite{caissie2006,isaak2017}. That number is
what enters a management judgement, and a station-year is admitted to an archive
or excluded from it on the strength of it. An error of one degree in a summer
peak converts a thermally stressful year into an unremarkable one; on our panel
that error is not hypothetical, because computing the peak from recorded days
alone carries a mean absolute error of 1.305\,$^{\circ}$C, larger than one
year-to-year standard deviation of the quantity itself.

The error of a derived statistic is not the same object as the error of the
series it is computed from, and the difference is what makes the admission
decision hard. A reconstruction with excellent daily skill can be badly wrong
about a 30-day maximum if its errors are correlated inside the window that
achieves the maximum, and a reconstruction with mediocre daily skill can recover
that maximum exactly if the window survived. Daily skill is what the modelling
literature reports; the derived statistic is what a manager reads. Between the
two sits a decision nobody writes down --- whether this station-year may be used
at all --- which is made for every record in every thermal archive, is made by a
rule, and is almost never made with the derived statistic in mind.

Two families of rule decide admission today, and they differ in what they assume
rather than in when they were written (Table~\ref{tab:map}). Agency collection
standards specify what must be present: \citeA{mauger2015}, writing minimum
standards for a state whose records feed regional analyses, require a minimum
sampling duration of one calendar month and recommend a deployment period of 1
June to 31 August ``to capture the thermal maxima''. Quantitative guidance
instead constrains what may be absent: \citeA{johnson2021} report that
``datasets with less than seven weeks of consecutively missing data enabled
estimation of all annual signal parameters with reasonable accuracy'' and that
``imputation of missing data expanded this range to approximately 20 weeks''.
The gap-filling literature optimises the accuracy of the filled series rather
than the admissibility of the completed year \cite{mohseni1998,li2017}, and the
closest prior work fills incomplete summer records so that a thermal peak can be
computed, but applies the filling uniformly and declines no station-year
\cite{beaufort2022}. Across all of them the conditioning variable is a property
of the record. None is a property of the statistic being computed from it, and
the transfer does happen in practice: \citeA{mauger2017}, for example, retain a
site-year for a mean July temperature only ``when at least 90\% of the days were
captured'', and then report the maximum weekly maximum temperature from the same
records.

That is the assumption we examine, and its consequence is not confined to a
prediction. An annual mean is a sum over days: removing $k$ days removes $k$
terms and it matters little which. A hottest 30-day mean is an extreme supported
on a short window: if the outage misses that window the statistic is recoverable
almost exactly however long the outage is, and if the outage covers it the
statistic must be inferred however short the outage is. A single completeness
threshold therefore cannot be correct for both, and the direction of its error
is opposite in the two cases. Applied to a summer extreme, a threshold on gap
length admits station-years whose peak was never observed and discards
station-years whose peak is intact; which of the two dominates is an empirical
question about the archive, not a matter of taste. The consequence is an archive
whose composition, and not only whose accuracy, depends on a choice nobody
records.

Three things make the question answerable now that were not available to the
studies that raised it. Water-temperature archives from nine national agencies
can be assembled into a panel large enough to hold out whole river networks, so
a screening rule can be scored on networks never used to build it rather than on
a resampling of the networks that built it. Because a fully observed calendar
year supplies a truth, a gap can be placed on it deliberately, which converts
the comparison between conditioning variables from an observational contrast
into a designed one: the two candidate properties of a gap can be varied
independently, which they never are in a record that failed on its own. And the
share of a statistic's support that a gap hides is computable at decision time
from the station's own earlier years, so the alternative conditioning variable
is not merely definable but deployable by an agency with no data it does not
already hold.

We therefore ask three linked questions, and the last matters most, because a
screening rule is not evaluated by its discrimination but by the archive it
leaves behind. First, which property of a data gap
governs the error of a derived thermal statistic, and does the answer depend on
which statistic is being computed? Second, if it does, is that dependence a
dichotomy between two regimes or a continuous transition, and where does it turn
over? Third, what does conditioning on the answer change --- for the error among
retained station-years, for which stations survive in the archive, and for the
2422 records in this same archive that really do have gaps and have never been
decided?

\section{Study system, data, and the quantity to be explained}

\subsection{The statistic, its support, and the share a gap hides}

Let a station-year be a sequence $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ of daily mean water
temperatures, $T\in\{365,366\}$, and let $q(\cdot)$ be the derived statistic of
interest. The family we sweep is the hottest consecutive $k$-day mean
\begin{equation}
q_k(y) \;=\; \max_{t}\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=t}^{t+k-1} y_i ,
\label{eq:qk}
\end{equation}
which is the annual maximum at $k=1$ and the annual mean at $k=T$; the coldest
30-day mean, the annual amplitude, and the sine-fit mean, phase and amplitude of
\citeA{johnson2021} are scored alongside.

A gap is a binary mask $m\in\{0,1\}^T$; the analyst observes the days where
$m_t=0$. A reconstruction $\hat y = R(y_{\bar m}, Z)$ completes the year using
side information $Z$ --- synchronous donor stations, air temperature, the
station's own history --- and reports $q(\hat y)$. The quantity that matters is
the error of the reported statistic,
\begin{equation}
L \;=\; \bigl| q(\hat y) - q(y) \bigr| ,
\end{equation}
and a station-year is called \emph{consequential} when $L>\tau$ for a tolerance
$\tau$ in the units of $q$.

The property of the gap we propose as the governing one follows from how much
each day moves the statistic. Writing $w^q_t = |\partial q(y)/\partial y_t|$ for
the discrete influence function of $q$ at $y$ \cite{hampel1974}, the
\emph{hidden mass} of a gap is the share of that influence it removes,
\begin{equation}
h_q(m) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{t:\, m_t=1} w^q_t}{\sum_t w^q_t}.
\label{eq:hidden}
\end{equation}
For the annual mean every $w_t$ is equal and $h_q$ is the missing fraction; for
the hottest $k$-day mean the influence is concentrated on the $k$ days achieving
the maximum and $h_q$ is the share of that window the gap hides; for the sine-fit
parameters the weights are smooth functions of day of year --- a constant for the
fitted mean, $|\sin(\theta_t-\phi)|$ for the amplitude, which is largest at both
extremes, and $|\cos(\theta_t-\phi)|$ for the phase, which is largest a
quarter-year from the peak. Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} therefore contains the
incumbent axis as a special case rather than opposing it, which is why the two
regimes reported below are ends of one scale.

Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} cannot be evaluated at decision time, because the
weights depend on the days the gap hides. What an analyst has is the
\emph{climatological} support: the window the station's other complete years say
the maximum should fall in. That estimate has an error, and
Section~\ref{sec:dilate} shows the error is large enough to change what a
screening rule should condition on.

\subsection{Records, and what each dataset is for}

Daily water-temperature series were assembled from nine national providers: ARSO
(Slovenia), CHMI (Czechia), FOEN (Switzerland), GKD Bayern and LUBW (Germany),
NVE (Norway), Rijkswaterstaat (Netherlands), SYKE (Finland) and the USGS (United
States). A \emph{network} is one provider's group of stations on a common river
system. It is the unit at which donor stations are available to one another, and
it is the clustering unit for every interval reported here, because station-years
within a network share weather, regulation and instrumentation.

Waterbody class was assigned per network and non-river objects --- seas, lakes,
canals, estuaries, deltas --- are excluded rather than averaged into a river
result. Reference series are never imputed: a calendar year with any missing day
supplies no truth. Stations with fewer than four fully observed calendar years
are excluded because the estimator protocol requires them. This leaves
\textbf{146 networks, 457 stations and 5114 fully observed station-years}.

The same 457 stations contribute \textbf{2422 further station-years that are
genuinely incomplete}. These carry no truth and are therefore not part of the
scored population; they are the population a screening rule exists for, and
Section~\ref{sec:deploy} decides them.

Station coordinates were assembled for all 457 stations, from the providers' own
catalogues, from the USGS NWIS site service for 195 United States gauges, and
from ARSO's public station feed, and daily 2\,m air temperature was joined from
NASA POWER for the whole panel, 1981 to 2026 \cite{nasapower2026}. For the
United States subset, basin characteristics were joined from GAGES-II
\cite{falcone2011} on the eight-digit gauge identifier, matching \textbf{140 of
204} eligible stations across 57 networks. Basin attributes are used to ask which
rivers are hard (Section~\ref{sec:hard}) and which rivers a screen keeps
(Section~\ref{sec:repr}); they are deliberately not used inside the screening
rule, because they are unavailable for the other eight agencies.

Table~\ref{tab:quantities} lists every quantity the analysis uses, what it means
physically, where its value comes from, and whether it was calibrated. Networks
--- never station-years --- were split into development and reserved, stratified
on provider and on donor regime, with the seed recorded before any scoring. This
gives \textbf{110 development and 36 reserved networks}. The reserved panel was
opened once, after the analysis was frozen, and the model was not retuned;
everything added after that opening is reported on development cases, with

\subsection{Gap design: two arms, and why both are needed}

A case is one station-year under one gap realization. Two arms are used and
reported separately, because they answer different questions and are not one
population. The \emph{empirical} arm samples real gap runs --- length and start
month --- from the same network's own records and transplants them onto fully
observed years, so that operational numbers reflect the geometry agencies
experience. The \emph{stratified} arm crosses gap length
$\{14,30,60,90,120,180,270\}$ days with the overlap between the gap and the
station's climatological peak window, so that the response surface is estimable
rather than accidental. The design yields \textbf{51524 cases}, 40704
development and 10820 reserved.

Both arms transplant a mask onto a year whose values were observed, which assumes
the mask is uninformative about the values it hides.
Section~\ref{sec:assume} tests that assumption against the archive rather than
asserting it. Gaps are placed inside one calendar year; failures that straddle
New Year damage two annual statistics while hiding neither warm season, and the
design cannot represent that case.

Each endpoint is scored against a tolerance equal to the same fraction, 0.47, of
\emph{its own} interannual standard deviation at the same stations. For the
annual peak that scale is 1.06\,$^{\circ}$C, giving a primary tolerance of
0.50\,$^{\circ}$C; the scale falls monotonically with support width, from
1.18\,$^{\circ}$C at $k=1$ to 0.55\,$^{\circ}$C at $k=365$. The fraction is swept
at 0.25, 0.47 and 1.0, and no conclusion about which axis wins is claimed unless
it is invariant across the sweep. The reference series carries instrument error
of roughly 0.1 to 0.2\,$^{\circ}$C, so the primary tolerance is about three times
sensor accuracy; Section~\ref{sec:assume} perturbs the reference by
0.15\,$^{\circ}$C and repeats the analysis.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Reconstruction, and what each estimator is allowed to see}

Every case is completed by each supported estimator: the statistic computed from
recorded days alone (current practice), a day-of-year median climatology, a
smoothed climatology, linear interpolation where both flanks exist, ridge
regression on the visible subset of synchronous donors, and an air-driven ridge
with lagged air temperature and harmonics. Estimators are fit leave-one-year-out
within the station, which is what offline archive repair permits;
Section~\ref{sec:assume} repeats the study under the stricter protocol in which
only prior years are available. Support is declared per method: donor regression
does not read a flanking observation, so requiring one would discard usable
cases, and absent donor columns are never imputed but the model is refit on the
visible subset.

One weakness in that library was worth removing before the comparison was run.
Air temperature was available for 2.3\% of development cases in an earlier
version of this analysis, so the reconstruction this field actually uses was
effectively absent, and 36 of the 110 development networks have no synchronous
donor at all. With coordinates joined for all 457 stations and NASA POWER pulled
for the whole panel, air forcing now covers 98.6\% of development cases and
100\% of reserved cases. The pre-registered confirmation is not re-scored
against the new forcing; Section~\ref{sec:fair} reports it as a
development-panel sensitivity analysis.

The reconstruction layer is not the contribution of this paper and is kept
deliberately simple. Its role is to make the comparison between conditioning
variables fair, which requires only that the estimator paired with the incumbent
rule is the strongest deployable one; Section~\ref{sec:fair} tests that
requirement directly rather than assuming it.

\subsection{The screening rule, and what it may not read}

For each case the screen predicts an upper bound on the absolute error each
supported estimator would produce, selects the estimator with the smallest
bound, and emits a conformal interval on the statistic together with an
admission decision at an analyst-chosen tolerance. Its inputs are the first six
rows of Table~\ref{tab:quantities}. No input may read a day the gap hides, and
that restriction is enforced in code rather than by convention.

Calibration is split conformal \cite{lei2018} with two properties that matter. It
is \emph{selection-conditional}, because taking the smallest of several bounds is
a winner's curse that per-estimator calibration cannot see, and
\emph{multiplicative}, because the error scale spans an order of magnitude
between a gap that misses the peak window and one that covers it. Fitting,
calibration and test networks are always disjoint.

\subsection{Test protocol}

Before any rule was fit we measured, on development cases, how much a rule with
perfect foresight could win inside each candidate action space, and compared it
with the margin the reserved panel can resolve. Two natural framings --- adopt or
reject a fixed reconstruction, and choose among reconstruction methods --- have
ceilings of $+0.038$ and $+0.080\,^{\circ}$C against a threshold of
$0.136\,^{\circ}$C, and were not pursued. Retain-or-exclude clears its gate by a
factor of five and is the action space studied here. A decision experiment whose
ceiling is below its own resolvable margin cannot produce evidence in either
direction, and running one consumes a reserved panel for nothing.

Every interval resamples whole networks, because station-years within a network
are not independent and reusing a station-year under several gap realizations
does not create independent observations. The pre-registered primary criterion,
frozen before the reserved panel was opened, is that at the retention of the
matched length rule the screen must reduce the consequential-error rate by at
least five percentage points with a network-clustered interval excluding zero.
Precision was computed in advance: the reserved panel resolves 1.44 points, so
the declared effect is 3.5 times the resolution.

Where several endpoints are compared, the direction of each contrast was
predicted in advance from the form of the statistic, which is the defence against
multiplicity. Three of those predictions were not met, and
Sections~\ref{sec:dilate} and \ref{sec:sine} report them as failures rather than
reshaping them into successes.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Computing the peak on recorded days alone costs more than a
year-to-year standard deviation}

Current practice is expensive before any rule is proposed. On development cases,
computing the annual peak from recorded days alone --- the default once a
station-year passes a completeness screen --- gives a mean absolute error of
\textbf{1.305\,$^{\circ}$C, or 1.23 interannual standard deviations} of the
quantity being measured, with 24.9\% of station-years wrong by more than
1.0\,$^{\circ}$C. Substituting a day-of-year climatology over the gap gives
0.402\,$^{\circ}$C, the best supported reconstruction 0.225\,$^{\circ}$C with air
forcing available for the whole panel (0.242\,$^{\circ}$C without it), and an
oracle over the estimator set 0.130\,$^{\circ}$C. The error is therefore
dominated not by which station-years are kept but by whether the record is
repaired at all, which fixes the size of the question the rest of the paper
addresses: given that a year has been repaired, which repaired years can be
trusted.

\subsection{The calibrated bound is honest enough to interpret}

Everything that follows uses a predicted error bound to rank station-years, so
the bound is checked before it is used. On the reserved networks the nominal 90\%
interval covers 90.9\% of cases marginally, inside the pre-declared band of 0.87
to 0.93, and 86.3\% to 94.9\% within every pre-declared stratum
(Figure~\ref{fig:calib}a). Median widths adapt with the quantity they are meant
to track, from 0.06\,$^{\circ}$C when the peak window is untouched to
1.87\,$^{\circ}$C when most of it is hidden.

Those strata pool across networks, and conformal validity assumes an
exchangeability that fails between river networks. Coverage inside individual
reserved networks has mean 0.904 and median 0.913 but a minimum of 0.778, with
five of 36 networks below 0.85. The pooled interval is an honest statement about
a population of networks and is \emph{not} a guarantee at a single new site;
calibrating on the per-network conformal quantiles at the 0.9 level puts no
network below 0.85 for a 1.5-fold widening. The screening results below are identical under every
calibration to four decimal places, because the rank order the screen uses is a
monotone transform of the bound, so a reader who distrusts the intervals need not
distrust the frontier.

synchronous anomaly information and not merely a seasonal shape.

\subsection{A support must be located before it can be conditioned on, so the
useful window is wider than the statistic}
\label{sec:dilate}

Two predictions declared for this round were not met, and both failures have one
cause. We predicted that the axis restricted to the estimand's own support would
be at least as good as any hand-chosen axis at every $k$, and that its advantage
would decay monotonically. Neither holds: the advantage is a hump peaking at a
60-day support, and for supports of 15 days or less the fixed 30-day peak window
beats the estimand's own support, by 0.064 AUC at $k=1$.

Table~\ref{tab:dilate} gives the reason. The gain from conditioning on a window
wider than the statistic tracks the year-to-year wander of the realised window's
location almost exactly: it is $+0.102$ AUC at $k=1$, where the climatological
window is the worst estimate of where this year's window will fall and the
wander is 16.7 days, and vanishes at $k\ge 90$, where the wander has fallen to
7.4 days and the support is wide enough to contain its own location error. A
screening rule therefore does not condition on the support; it conditions on the
support \emph{dilated by the uncertainty in the support's location}, and a
dilation of roughly two location standard deviations on each side is what the
data choose.

\subsection{On the estimands the standing guidance was derived for, the guidance

\label{sec:sine}

\citeA{johnson2021} fit a sine wave by linear regression and take a mean, a phase

overlap by $+0.265$ (0.249, 0.285).

\subsection{Topography, not geology, makes a river hard}
\label{sec:hard}

The account so far is a property of estimators and estimands and would hold for
any time series. For the 140 United States stations matched to GAGES-II across 57
networks, we regressed donor synchrony and completed-year error on basin
attributes with the expected direction of each effect declared before the
regressions were run (Figure~\ref{fig:hydro}). Site elevation and basin slope
both reduce donor synchrony (Spearman $\rho=-0.29$, 95\% CI $-0.46$ to $-0.08$,
and $\rho=-0.31$, $-0.51$ to $-0.07$), and site elevation raises both
completed-year error ($\rho=+0.24$, 0.06 to 0.43) and the consequential-error
rate ($\rho=+0.25$, 0.08 to 0.43).

The groundwater prediction taken from the literature is not reproduced, and the
reason is instructive. \citeA{johnson2021} report that filling can harm
groundwater-influenced sites, which predicts larger completed-year error at a
high baseflow index. Baseflow index does reduce donor synchrony in the predicted
direction, from 0.70 in the lowest tertile to 0.59 in the highest, although the
rank correlation interval includes zero ($\rho=-0.19$, $-0.42$ to 0.10). But the
error moves the other way: high-baseflow stations are $-0.043\,^{\circ}$C easier
than low-baseflow stations (95\% CI $-0.081$ to $-0.004$). Across baseflow
tertiles the mean annual peak falls from 22.7\,$^{\circ}$C to 18.1\,$^{\circ}$C
($\rho=-0.47$, $-0.60$ to $-0.26$) and its interannual standard deviation from
0.98 to 0.76\,$^{\circ}$C (Figure~\ref{fig:hydro}c), so two effects act in
opposite directions and the second is larger.

Regulation does not make station-years harder for this endpoint: reference and
non-reference basins differ by $+0.004\,^{\circ}$C (95\% CI $-0.051$ to
$+0.093$). Catchment attributes explain a modest share of the variation, a
standardised linear model reaching $R^2=0.22$ for donor synchrony and 0.13 for
completed-year error, and no attribute predicts how much a screen gains: every
correlation between a basin attribute and the per-station screening benefit has
an interval containing zero. Whether an attribute predicts what a screen
\emph{keeps} is a different question, and Section~\ref{sec:repr} answers it
differently.

\subsection{On records that really have gaps, the two rules disagree on one year
in six}
\label{sec:deploy}

Every number above is computed on a complete station-year with a synthetic hole
punched in it, which is the only way to have a truth. The same archive contains
2422 genuinely incomplete station-years, a third of the total, which carry no
truth and have never been decided. Three things can be asked of them without
inventing one (Figure~\ref{fig:deploy}).

They are not a marginal population. The median incomplete year is missing 9 days
and the 90th percentile 128; 28.7\% of them have at least one day of the
station's climatological peak window missing, and 261 have more than half of it
missing.

The two rules disagree on a sixth of them. At the seven-week unfilled limit the
length rule admits 83.2\% and a peak-window rule at 10\% hidden admits 81.2\% ---
near-equal retention, which is the comparison this paper insists on --- and they
\textbf{disagree on 15.1\%} of station-years: 207 are admitted by the incumbent
and rejected on the support, and 158 are rejected by the incumbent and admitted

\subsection{The conclusion survives instrument error, a stricter fitting
protocol, and transfer across agencies}
\label{sec:assume}

\textbf{Missingness is weakly informative, and the effect is bounded well below
the tolerance.} Every case transplants a mask onto a year whose values were
observed, which is valid only if the mask is ignorable in the sense of
\citeA{rubin1976}; loggers are lost to floods, buried by ice and fouled in low
flow, and each of those coincides with anomalous conditions. Across the 6472
real gaps, the mean anomaly of the seven days preceding a gap, relative to the
station's own day-of-year climatology, is $+0.018\,^{\circ}$C for gaps of a week
or less ($p=0.44$), $+0.038$ for 8 to 29 days ($p=0.56$), $-0.002$ for 30 to 89
days ($p=0.98$) and $-0.215$ for 90 days and longer ($p=0.081$). Donors recording
through a gap run $+0.063\,^{\circ}$C warm during short gaps, detectable at this
sample size ($p=0.017$) and 3\% of the within-station anomaly scale. The design
is therefore defended on the magnitude of the effect rather than on its absence,
and the class that warrants a limitation is gaps of three months and longer.

\textbf{Three further checks move the numbers and not the conclusion.}
Perturbing the reference series by 0.15\,$^{\circ}$C of normal noise moves the
development contrast from 6.32 to 6.21 points, the reserved contrast from 7.06 to
6.67 points, and the mean absolute error from 0.2416 to 0.2418\,$^{\circ}$C. Restricting estimators to years before the gap
raises reconstruction error from 0.242 to 0.270\,$^{\circ}$C on a panel that
falls to 33633 development cases, and moves the contrast from $-6.35$ ($-7.12$,
$-5.52$) to $-5.92$ points ($-6.72$, $-4.99$) with coverage 0.906. Under
leave-one-provider-out the contrast is negative with an interval excluding zero
for all nine agencies, from $-11.6$ points (Finland) to $-1.2$ (Switzerland),
with conformal coverage between 0.82 and 0.96.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What process explains the transition}

The transition measured in Table~\ref{tab:sweep} is not a property of rivers, of
the estimators, or of the screening rule. It is a property of the functional
being computed, and it follows from where that functional puts its weight. A
statistic whose influence is concentrated on a few days is destroyed by losing
those days and is almost unaffected by losing any others; a statistic whose
influence is spread evenly over the year is degraded in proportion to how many
days go missing and is indifferent to which. Between those two limits the
influence is neither concentrated nor uniform, and the discriminating power of a
narrow conditioning window falls smoothly as the weight it captures falls. The
crossing at 91 days is where a fixed 30-day window stops capturing more influence
than a whole-year count does.

Two measurements distinguish that account from a re-description of the data.
First, the gradient ratios swap by roughly an order of magnitude in each
direction between the peak and the annual mean, which is a direct measurement of
how error grows along each axis rather than an inference from discrimination.
Second, and more decisively, the account makes a prediction about a case it was
not built on. The sine-fit phase has an influence function with two broad lobes a
quarter-year from the peak, so the account predicts that peak-window overlap
should be a poor axis for it --- and it is, at 0.583 against 0.836 for gap length.
A description fitted to the extreme-versus-average contrast would not have
predicted that.

The same account explains where it failed. We predicted that conditioning on the
estimand's own support would always be best, and it is not, because the support
has to be estimated and the estimate wanders. Once the wander is measured the
failure becomes a quantitative statement rather than an exception: the gain from
widening the conditioning window tracks the wander from 16.7 days at $k=1$ to 7.4
days at $k=90$ and disappears when the support becomes wide enough to contain it.
This is the part of the result we would expect to transfer to other variables and
other climates, because nothing in it is specific to water temperature: any
estimator conditioned on an estimated support inherits the uncertainty of that
support.

\subsection{Why this agrees with the standing guidance rather than against it}

An earlier framing of this work held that the incumbent rule gets an ordering
backwards. Section~\ref{sec:sine} shows that reading is wrong on the guidance's
own terms. \citeA{johnson2021} derive their limits for sine-fit mean, phase and
amplitude, and for those three estimands a completeness threshold is the correct
axis or within 0.02 AUC of it. What we correct is not the threshold but the habit
of carrying it across to an estimand it was not derived for.

That reading also resolves the anomaly the guidance itself reported. Its authors
observed that the timing of missing data affected thermal-signal estimates
``inconsistently in space and time''. Their three estimands have three different
influence functions --- a constant, two lobes at the extremes, two lobes at the
transitions --- and only one of the three has weight concentrated narrowly enough
for a timing effect to appear. Observing all three at once, inconsistency is the
only thing one could see. We do not contradict the observation; we decompose it,
and we can now say in advance which of their parameters should show a timing
effect and which should not.

A smaller result points the same way and was not expected. The fixed
information-conditioned policy gets slightly \emph{worse} at donor-less stations
once air forcing becomes available, because its preference order was written when
air was rare and it now prefers an air regression to a climatology at sites where
the climatology is better. A fixed ladder over estimators is not robust to a
change in what is available; a per-case selection is. That is an argument for the
method of this paper rather than against it, and we report it because it was the
opposite of what we expected.

The disagreement with the gap-filling literature is of a different kind and is
not resolved by our result. \citeA{beaufort2022} fill every record alike, and on
our panel pairing the incumbent rule with that treatment makes it worse rather
than better (Table~\ref{tab:fair}). That is not evidence against
air-temperature regression, which is the best available estimator at many of our
stations and which the screen selects for 20.0\% of cases. It is evidence that a
reconstruction chosen once for a whole archive and a reconstruction chosen per
station-year are different objects, and that the quantity a screening comparison
must hold fixed is the second.

\subsection{What generalises, and what does not}

Three parts of the result are portable. The governing quantity of
Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} is defined wherever a derived statistic is computed
from an incomplete record, and its ingredients --- an influence function and a
mask --- are available in any such setting. The protocol that measures the
transition is portable in the same way: compute the statistic over a family of
support widths, measure how far the support's location moves between periods,
dilate by that amount, and condition on the result. And the finding that a
screening rule must be specified together with its strata is a statement about
selection, not about temperature.

Three parts are not. The numerical crossover at 91 days depends on how
concentrated a river's thermal year is and on how far its peak wanders between
years; both are properties of this climate and this variable, and we would expect
the crossover to move in a system with a flatter seasonal cycle or a more mobile
peak. The operational gain is regime-dependent and is not resolvable under the
gap geometry these networks currently experience (Section~\ref{sec:absent}), so
the headline contrast should not be quoted as what an agency will obtain. And the
catchment attribution rests on 140 of 204 stations in one country, so the
statement that topography rather than geology makes a river hard is a statement
about the United States subset of this panel.

One boundary deserves naming separately, because it limits interpretation rather
than transfer. The mechanism argument is supported by an influence-function
derivation, by measured error gradients, by a continuum with a located crossover
and by an out-of-sample prediction about the phase. It is not supported by an
independent observation of the internal quantity itself, because the hidden mass
of Equation~\eqref{eq:hidden} is by construction unobservable on the records it
matters for. A different representation that reproduced the same gradients would
not be excluded by our evidence.

\subsection{What the remaining uncertainty changes, and what it does not}

Uncertainty enters this study in four places, and it is worth separating what
each one moves. Measurement error in the reference series changes the numbers in
the third decimal and changes neither the mechanism nor the recommendation:
0.15\,$^{\circ}$C of noise moves the reserved contrast from 7.06 to 6.67 points.
Fitting protocol changes the numbers by about half a point and changes nothing
else: restricting estimators to prior years moves the contrast from $-6.35$ to
$-5.92$. Neither of these is capable of overturning a conclusion.

The other two are. The choice of population changes the practical conclusion
completely: on the arm built from the gap geometry these networks actually
experience the effect is $-0.71$ points with an interval containing zero, and any
reader who needs a number for their own archive needs the arm that matches their
gap regime rather than the pooled figure. And the absence of an independent
observation of the internal quantity limits the mechanism judgement, as the
previous section states: we can show that conditioning on an estimate of the
support discriminates better, and we cannot show that no other representation
would do as well.

Two further limits belong here because they are specific rather than generic.
Missingness is weakly informative and the transplant design is defended on the
magnitude of that effect rather than on its absence, which makes the defence
weakest for gaps of 90 days and longer, where the leading flank runs
$0.215\,^{\circ}$C cold. And the archive-level consequence is small: the choice
of rule moves a regional mean annual peak by less than 0.1\,$^{\circ}$C. We
report that because it deflates a claim we might have made. At the level of a
regional aggregate the decision that matters is whether to repair; the rule
matters per station-year, and it matters for who is in the archive.

\subsection{What changes in the judgement an agency actually makes}
\label{sec:judgement}

The archive-level aggregate is the wrong place to look for the value of a
screening rule, because it averages over the station-years where the call is
easy. A criterion-exceedance judgement is the right place, because it is made
one station-year at a time and it is where a wrong peak becomes a wrong
decision. On reserved networks the misclassification rate of a
20\,$^{\circ}$C exceedance is 6.86\% when the statistic is computed on recorded
days, 1.97\% when every year is reconstructed and not screened, 1.53\% under
length screening and 0.36\% under the screen. Restricted to the years where the
call is close the four figures are 16.84\%, 9.50\%, 7.26\% and 1.92\% within
1.0\,$^{\circ}$C of the threshold and 19.10\%, 13.12\%, 10.24\% and 3.38\%
within 0.5\,$^{\circ}$C, so the advantage is proportionally larger exactly where
it is needed. The bias in the count of years
above 20\,$^{\circ}$C falls from $-0.76$ years per station under recorded days
and $-0.32$ under length screening to $+0.06$.

That result is not an artefact of the threshold chosen. Swept from 14 to
24\,$^{\circ}$C the screen's misclassification stays between 0.18\% and 0.37\%
while the length rule stays between 0.84\% and 1.53\%. The sweep matters because
no single number is the criterion: regulatory practice uses a seven-day average
of daily maxima with criteria differing by several degrees between jurisdictions
and designated uses, 16\,$^{\circ}$C for core cold-water salmonid habitat and
13\,$^{\circ}$C for salmonid spawning \cite{wac2024}, which is why the seven-day
endpoint is reported throughout and why the threshold is swept rather than
asserted.

For a judgement of this kind, screening is not the best use of the calibrated
object. Discarding a station-year is a blunt response to uncertainty about a
single call: screening at the 0.5\,$^{\circ}$C tolerance judges 67.5\% of years
at 0.36\% misclassification, whereas abstaining only where the interval straddles
the threshold judges 92.7\% at 0.42\%, and doing both judges 65.7\% at 0.08\%.
Keeping 93\% of station-years rather than 68\% at essentially the same error rate
matters to anyone assembling a regional archive. The two actions answer different
questions --- screening decides what enters an archive, abstention decides what
one judgement may say --- and the same calibrated object serves both, which is an
argument for calibrating rather than for screening.

\subsection{What an agency should change, and in what order}

The first recommendation is not to adopt a model and not even to change a
screening rule. It is to look at the maintenance calendar. Holding the real
distribution of gap lengths fixed and changing only when gaps begin, a calendar
concentrated in March to July would raise the consequential-error rate of the
annual peak from 8.7\% to 13.2\%, and one concentrated in September to January
would lower it to 4.9\%: swings of $+4.4$ and $-3.9$ percentage points against a
screening gain of 6.3 points at 70\% retention. That is the same order of
magnitude, and it is available before any data are lost rather than after. Our
archives sit near the good end of that range for gaps of three months and more
and near the indifferent middle for everything shorter, so the headroom is real.
Servicing loggers, replacing batteries and scheduling site visits in autumn
converts recoverable gaps into harmless ones; doing it in late spring does the
reverse. Redundancy follows the same logic: a second station is worth having in
proportion to its synchronous anomaly correlation with the target rather than its
mere existence, and synchrony is lowest at high-elevation, steep sites, which is
where a redundant gauge buys the most.

The second recommendation is to change what the rule conditions on, and the third
is to stratify the change. An agency screening station-years for warm-season
extremes on maximum gap length should condition instead on how much of the
station's climatological warm window the gap hides, widened to 60 to 90 days
rather than the 30 days of the metric itself, because one window of that width
serves every warm-season endpoint and absorbs the year-to-year wander of the
peak's position. On our reserved panel that substitution takes the
consequential-error rate from 9.7\% to 4.2\% at equal retention with three
variables and to 2.5\% with the calibrated screen. But the threshold should be
applied within strata, not globally, or the archive quietly loses its mountain
stations at a cost of 0.31 points to prevent. Equally, agencies should
\emph{keep} their length-based rules for annual means, cold extremes and the
sine-fit regime parameters the standing guidance was written for.

Two cautions bound the recommendation. Nothing here applies to ungauged sites,
because the rule uses a station's own history to locate the support. And a
calibrated interval is not a deployment guarantee: the pooled interval is valid
for a population of networks and not at a single new site unless the cluster
calibration is used, which costs about a 1.5-fold widening. An agency that needs
a site-level statement should pay that price explicitly rather than read the
pooled interval as one.

\section{Conclusions}

Whether a data gap matters depends on how much the statistic being computed
depends on the days it hides. Measuring that share --- the hidden mass on the
statistic's support --- orders the regimes a completeness threshold cannot see,
and it does so continuously: sweeping the support of a warm-season mean from one
day to a full year, the advantage of a peak-window axis over a completeness axis
decays from $+0.074$ AUC and crosses zero at 91 days. Because a support must be
located before it can be conditioned on, and a river's hottest window wanders by
one to two weeks between years, the conditioning set that works is the support
widened by that wander, and one climatological window of 60 to 90 days is
near-optimal for every warm-season endpoint we examined.

Three conditions bound that statement. On the sine-fit estimands the standing
guidance was derived for, the same account says the guidance is right, so the
correction is to the transfer of a threshold and not to the threshold. The
operational gain is regime-dependent: under the gap calendar these agencies
currently keep it is not resolvable, because gaps long enough to destroy a summer
peak already begin in autumn. And a rule that conditions on difficulty selects
against high, steep, donor-poor catchments unless its threshold is applied within
strata.

What follows for practice is correspondingly specific. Keep length-based
completeness rules for averages and for sine-fit regime parameters; replace them
with support-based rules for warm-season extremes, using a window of 60 to 90
days rather than the width of the metric; report retention by catchment attribute
alongside the error rate, because a rule that removes the mountain stations from
a national thermal archive has done damage no error rate will show; and schedule
the loggers, because on this panel when a gauge fails is worth as much as which
records are discarded afterwards.
