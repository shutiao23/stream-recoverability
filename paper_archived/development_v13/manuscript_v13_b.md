# Historical block stress tests rank future model-specific reconstruction error across stream-temperature networks

## Abstract

The recovery loss of a missing stream-temperature block depends on the model that fills it, so we framed prediction as model-conditional historical stress testing: cut seasonally stratified artificial gaps inside the fitting years of each record, score the intended recovery model on them, and use the resulting curve to rank that model's future error. Development used 55 river networks; a first panel of 42 networks supported post-outcome method development; a second, outcome-disjoint panel of 57 networks (32 US, 15 Czech, 10 Norwegian) provided evaluation. On 874 directly supported units (7, 30, 90, 180 days), the stress curve ranked later loss at pooled Spearman 0.945 and network-level 0.805. The strongest fitting-record comparator, the station-by-horizon mean of the network's own trial gaps, nearly matched it (pooled 0.942; paired network difference +0.042, 95% CI [-0.0006, +0.1154]); simple descriptors ranked below. Exact station-season curves carried the signal; interpolated durations stayed well calibrated; 365-day extrapolation failed (rank 0.270; 90% coverage 46.8%). Difficulty ordering transferred across engineered-regression families but not across architecture families (the neural self-transfer cell is cross-instance transfer), and two independent missingness-mechanism implementations disagreed, so that matrix is not yet a claim. For fixed-budget triage the frozen predictor was the worst non-random policy (CapturedLoss@20% 0.338 versus 0.512), and support-gated model selection reached regret 0.0067 only at 8.5% coverage (123 units, 8 networks). Historical stress tests are model-conditional screening tools whose decision release requires support checks and independent preregistered validation. (235 words)

## 1. Introduction

A failed logger can leave weeks or months of missing stream temperature while boundary observations, neighboring stations, weather records, and discharge remain available. The operational task is not abstract imputation accuracy: a gap has occurred, its boundaries and donor rosters are visible, and someone must choose what to do before the missing truth exists. The default handling is itself a decision—drop the gap days, fill with climatology, or interpolate across the boundaries—and each default carries its own downstream distortion. A monitoring manager therefore needs a pre-outcome estimate of how much recovery error a specific gap will cause for the specific reconstruction model the agency will run, and a rule for when that estimate is trustworthy enough to release. This paper develops and evaluates one instrument for that task.

Gap-filling studies usually ask which model reconstructs missing values best [@li2017streamairimputation; @bal2023streamtemperature], benchmarking families on test windows under uniform, random missingness. A separate uncertainty literature provides monitoring-design and risk surfaces: Gaussian posterior variance, kriging variance, mutual information, and value of information have long been used to place sensors and gauges [@caselton1984monitoring; @pardo1998gauges; @krause2008sensor; @alfonso2012voi], with recent hydrological design using rank-revealing QR decomposition validated by reconstruction [@oh2025sensors], and flux-network studies using stratified artificial gaps to show that uncertainty grows with gap length and season [@moffat2007gap; @richardson2007longgaps]. What these traditions do not supply is a quantitative link between a pre-outage risk estimate and the realized recovery loss of a specific model on unseen river networks: analytic variance is rarely validated against realized model error, and trial-gap experiments are rarely carried through to rank or decision metrics on outcome-disjoint panels. Uncertainty metrics can mislead precisely because they are not realized model error—classical kriging variance can understate prediction variance when covariance parameters are estimated [@denhertog2006kriging], a value-independent variance need not represent local error dispersion [@yamamoto2000kriging], and hydrological conformal prediction shows that calibrated intervals still may not cover local extremes [@auer2024uncertainty].

The knowledge gap is prospective and model-specific. A reconstruction family's error on a given gap is a property of that family's fit, its features, and its sensitivity to the outage, not a property of the gap alone; and the only evidence available before a future gap is the fitting record. Fitting-period model failures—artificial gaps cut into the already-observed years and scored with the intended recovery model—are the natural prospective evidence for that same model's error on a future gap, because they inherit the model's own error structure, local donor behavior, and seasonal dynamics. Whether such fitting-period failures actually predict later loss, and where that predictive relationship breaks (by support, horizon, model family, and missingness mechanism), is untested. Trial gaps themselves are established tools [@moffat2007gap; @richardson2007longgaps]; our contribution is the model-conditional evaluation and its decision consequences.

The design is within-network historical stress testing replicated across an outcome-disjoint network panel. For each network, seasonally stratified artificial gaps are placed wholly inside the fitting years and recovered with the intended model; the resulting station-by-duration-by-season curve is the predictor. Because the curve is measured on the same stations, seasons, and recovery model as the evaluation gaps, it is an explicit proxy for the model's future error. Support is explicit rather than binary: exact local curves, station- and network-duration fallbacks, a network-mean fallback, and a continuous support-aware risk surface that interpolates unsupported durations and flags extrapolation. The primary fitting-record comparator is the station-by-horizon historical mean—the strongest baseline available to a manager without any new machinery—and the decision framing is incremental benefit over a stated default handling, not raw rank magnitude. Three recovery-model families, a neural model, a process model, seven missingness mechanisms, and two decision experiments delimit the claim.

We ask three questions. RQ1: does model-specific historical stress predict future recovery loss beyond the strongest fitting-record baseline—the station-by-horizon historical mean—on the same units, and how large is the incremental value? RQ2: how do support tier, gap duration, model family, missingness mechanism, and environmental shift bound that predictability, including where interpolation succeeds and extrapolation fails? RQ3: does a support-aware selector reduce recovery-model regret and downstream thermal distortion at prespecified coverage, relative to each untreated default? The river network is the primary extrapolation unit throughout (Figure 1).

## 2. Methods

Evidence-role labels used throughout: **[frozen]** = analyses fixed before outcome scoring (the first-panel analyses and the second panel; internally hash-bound, but amendment and outcomes entered version control in the same commit, so not externally verifiable preregistration); **[dev13]** = post-hoc v13 development analyses on already-scored panels (all revision analyses t01–t12; none is independent confirmation); **[pre-reg]** = future preregistered protocol (Section 2.11).

### 2.1 Study phases and data

The development collection combined 55 river networks, 217 stations, and 1,260 station-gap units for which temperature, meteorology, hydraulics, analytic risk, and recovery outcome used identical fitting rosters. The first confirmation panel contained 42 scoreable networks from North America and Europe contributing 1,440 station-gap units; it was used for post-confirmation method development (all results reported here that use it are [dev13]). The second, outcome-disjoint panel scored 57 networks—32 United States, 15 Czech, and 10 Norwegian—contributing 1,446 units (verified from the scoring artifact `predictions.csv`; the earlier manuscript statement "35 US" was an error and is corrected here); no second-panel network appeared in the development or first-panel outcome sets [frozen]. Daily mean stream temperature was the target. Calendar years were ordered and the first 70% used for fitting; the remaining years supplied observed truth for 7-, 14-, 30-, 60-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day artificial gaps, with up to 20 placements per station-gap cell. The primary loss was mean absolute error (MAE) in degrees Celsius. Stations and gaps were repeated observations nested within networks.

### 2.2 Recovery-model roster

The prespecified recovery condition was gradient boosting (300 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.05) with local gap boundaries and synchronous donor temperatures [frozen]. To separate information availability from model-specific error, the same gaps were scored with two additional engineered families: a seasonal-boundary ridge regression (three annual harmonics and linear interpolation between observed gap boundaries) and a donor covariance regression (synchronous neighboring temperatures with ridge stabilization). Median feature imputation, scaling, and all coefficients were fit using fitting years only.

Two further families delimit model conditionality [dev13]. A properly regularized mask-aware bidirectional LSTM (hidden size 16, early stopping on a nested fitting-period validation split, patience 12, three seeds, 12 networks) trained to convergence (median best epoch 68; 28% of runs hit the epoch cap, versus 92.9% of networks for the older bounded run, which was limited to five epochs and did not converge). We also implemented the published eight-parameter air2stream state equation and Crank–Nicolson update [@toffolon2015air2stream] in Python, calibrated on train-only bounded multistart least squares; the outcome-blind subset was eight second-panel US networks with 89 station-gap units. The air2stream equivalent is US-only, and POWER local-solar versus USGS local-civil daily windows share date labels but not identical 24-hour boundaries.

### 2.3 Model-conditional empirical transfer and support tiers

We nested a second chronological split inside the outer fitting years: the first 70% of those years fit the same gradient-boosting recovery family; the remaining fitting years supplied observed artificial-gap truth. For 7-, 30-, 90-, and 180-day gaps, candidate starts were divided into DJF, MAM, JJA, and SON seasons and up to 20 placements selected per season; their mean MAE formed a station-by-duration-by-season empirical curve before the outer evaluation period began. Each prediction carries one of five support tiers, from most to least specific: exact local support (station x duration x season curve), station-duration support (season collapsed), network-duration support (station collapsed), network-mean fallback (a network-wide mean of all fitting-period losses), and unavailable. Units at unsupported horizons (14, 60, 365 days) always fall to the network-mean tier. No outer evaluation loss entered any source. The curve is model-conditional: it is built with the same recovery model whose future error it ranks.

### 2.4 Continuous support-aware risk surface

To replace the network-mean fallback with a prediction at any duration, we fit a hierarchical model of fitting-period MAE on the pooled development and first-panel fitting placements (100,397 rows, 93 networks, 376 stations): log(1 + MAE) ~ network random intercept + station(network) random intercept + exactly monotone quadratic B-spline f(log duration) + two cyclic Fourier harmonics of day-of-year + seven station covariates (temperature SD and IQR, climatology error, lag-1 and lag-gap autocorrelation, daily gradient, donor R2), estimated by exact REML [dev13]. The second panel's 57 networks are new levels, so their random effects shrink to zero: every second-panel prediction is pure transfer of the shared surface. Predictions at 14 and 60 days are interpolations of the monotone duration curve; the 365-day horizon is flagged extrapolation, and its 90% log-scale Gaussian interval is widened by a factor of 1.435. We evaluated the surface on the second panel and cross-checked it by refitting on first-panel fit losses only.

### 2.5 Strongest-baseline ladder and the station-by-horizon historical mean

To place the empirical curve against the strongest information that a manager already holds, we constructed a baseline ladder on identical units (same-unit paired comparisons) [dev13]: r1 global mean; r2 gap-length mean; r3 gap x season mean; r4 network historical mean (fitting-period); r5 network x horizon mean; r6 station x horizon mean—the mean of the network's own fitting-period trial losses in the station's horizon cell; r7 previous-period leave-one-out gap x season; r8 simple structural descriptors (route-A, fit-period: autocorrelation, donor R2, gap-length heuristics, seasonal harmonics); r10 generic leave-one-network-out blocked-CV mean; r11 the hierarchical surface; r12 a meta-model stack of r8 + r11; and the empirical curve itself. **r6 is the primary fitting-record comparator** throughout: it is the strongest non-proposed rung on the direct 874-unit subset, it uses only the same fitting-period information available to the empirical curve, and it is trivially deployable. All paired differences were computed on identical unit subsets and bootstrapped with 2,000 draws resampling networks (Section 2.10).

### 2.6 Downstream thermal-regime metrics and incremental-benefit framing

On 15 first-panel networks, each reconstruction was inserted into the evaluation-period daily record and ten thermal-regime metrics recomputed: annual mean, summer (JJA) mean, amplitude (July minus January mean), phase (day of peak), 90th percentile, summer maximum, days above 20 and 25 C, degree days above 10 C, and trend slope; distortion was the absolute difference from the truth record. Because the value of reconstruction depends on what would have happened without it, the design specifies three untreated defaults—**no-fill** (drop gap days), **climatology** (day-of-year climatology fill), and **linear boundary interpolation**—and frames all downstream claims as incremental benefit B = D(default) - D(model) - lambda C, where D is aggregate metric distortion, C is intervention cost, and lambda is the shadow price of the action. Two independent implementations were completed [dev13]: implementation A (1,965 placements, 131 stations, horizons 7/30/90) scored the climatology default; implementation B (1,755 placements, 117 stations, 351 station-gaps, horizons 7/30/90) scored both the no-fill and climatology defaults. Their panels and placement rosters differ, so they are reported side by side rather than pooled; harmonization of the downstream panel is required before any single number can be quoted (see Section 3.5).

### 2.7 Missingness-mechanism matrix and harmonization status

On first-panel networks, trial gaps were generated with a mechanism's own placement distribution and recovered with the same XGBoost family, building a mechanism-matched station-by-horizon curve; evaluation gaps used the same mechanism. Mechanisms were: uniform single block; multi-block; summer-biased; high-temperature-biased; donor-synchronous (target and all donors masked); target-plus-primary-covariate (implementation A) or target-plus-air-temperature forcing outage (implementation B); and online left-boundary recovery. A mismatch experiment applied the uniform-block curve to every other mechanism's evaluation gaps. **Harmonization status**: two independent implementations (A, B) exist and disagree; they use different 12-network panels (six networks in common) and different forcing definitions for the covariate/forcing mechanism, and they report different matched and mismatched transfer values (Section 3.7). Because the two implementations have not been reconciled, the missingness matrix is Supporting Information material, not a main claim, and no single matched-transfer value—including the A-implementation donor-synchronous 0.979—may be cited as evidence until the panels, mechanism definitions, and scoring are harmonized.

### 2.8 Decision experiments

**Part 1, fixed-budget triage [dev13]**: on the second panel (1,446 units, 57 networks), units were ranked by seven scores—raw gap length, a duration-plus-season fit, the simple-descriptor model, the frozen empirical predictor, the hierarchical surface, random, and an observed-loss oracle—and the top 5, 10, 20, and 30% of units awarded their observed loss. CapturedLoss@B was the fraction of total loss in the top-B set; NDCG@B the position-discounted normalized gain; paired differences used a 2,000-draw network bootstrap. This experiment is reported as a **diagnostic negative** for the frozen predictor.

**Part 2, coverage-floored model selection [dev13]**: on the first panel (1,440 units, 42 networks), for each of three recovery families (seasonal-boundary ridge, donor ridge, XGBoost), a per-family fitting-period stress curve was built (unit-level curves where placements exist, pooled duration curves otherwise), recalibrated to the outer-loss scale, and penalized by family-specific interval width (lambda in {0, 0.5, 1}). The selector picked the family with the lowest penalized risk and could abstain when (i) the two best penalized risks were within 10% (ambiguity) or (ii) any family lacked unit-level stress support (support_any) or the winning family lacked it (support_winner). Regret was the network-balanced mean of (selected loss - best-family loss); comparators were best-fixed-family, global leave-one-network-out CV, per-network average CV, a gap-length rule, and random. Coverage (fraction of units and networks released after abstention) is reported with every regret value; the v13 design requires that any positive selection claim meet prespecified coverage floors (Section 2.11).

### 2.9 Rolling-origin, history length, and training-length comparability

Ranking stability was tested by moving the outer chronological cutoff to 60, 70, and 80% of each record on a 20-network first-panel subset, rebuilding the stress curve strictly inside each training block (10 placements per season), and comparing predicted network ranks across cutoffs with Kendall's W. A history-length learning curve fit the stress model on the first 2, 4, 6, 8, and full years of the record on 20 networks with fixed evaluation windows. Training-length comparability was tested by building deployment-matched curves with the full 70% training block and comparing them with the standard 49%-length stress curves on the same cells. All of these are [dev13]; two independent implementations (A, B) produced slightly different per-cutoff Spearman values under different pooling scopes, and both are reported in Section 3.3.

### 2.10 Inference, calibration, and intervals

All predictions left out complete networks. Results report network-level Spearman (over network means) first, followed by pooled station-gap and within-network rank as diagnostics. Calibration intercept and slope used weights inversely proportional to the number of station-gap rows in each network. Network bootstrap intervals resampled rivers (2,000 draws); same-unit paired comparisons computed both predictors on identical unit subsets and paired the bootstrap differences within resampled networks.

### 2.11 Evidence roles and the validation protocol (protocol v4)

The second panel was internally frozen and hash-bound before its outcomes were scored, but because its amendment and outcomes entered version control in the same commit, it is not externally verifiable preregistration; all revision analyses (t01–t12) are post-hoc v13 development on already-scored panels and are not independent confirmation. Protocol v3 [dev13 draft] fixed the provenance flaw (separate pre-outcome commit, external OSF/Zenodo registration, outcome commit referencing the registration; target 80–120 outcome-disjoint networks; binding primary endpoint paired network-level DeltaRho on direct-support units, observed +0.038, 80% power at N = 120) but its endpoint and margins were chosen before the decision results below were known. **Protocol v4 (paper/development_v13/protocol_v4.md) therefore replaces v3** with a primary endpoint chosen on decision value rather than rank magnitude: the fixed-coverage, network-balanced **selection regret difference** between a support-aware selector with abstention and the strongest deployable comparator, evaluated with **coverage floors of at least 50% of units and at least 60% of networks** released, with power recomputed from the Part-2 effect (regret 0.0067 on released units versus 0.151–0.164 for comparators) and from the abstention-coverage trade-off. All margins and floors are frozen before third-panel outcomes; amendments require a new external registration.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Strongest-baseline external comparison

On the 874 second-panel units at the four directly supported horizons (7, 30, 90, 180 days), the frozen empirical predictor reached station-gap (pooled) Spearman 0.945, network-level Spearman 0.805, and equal-network calibration slope 0.938 [frozen]. **The station-by-horizon historical mean (r6)—the strongest fitting-record comparator—nearly matched it**: pooled Spearman 0.942 (0.9424) versus 0.945 (0.9453), network-level 0.763 versus 0.805, with the two predictors correlating 0.992 on these units [dev13]. The same-unit paired difference within the 2,000-draw network bootstrap was small at the pooled level (+0.003, 95% CI [-0.0004, +0.0068]) and directionally positive but **straddling zero** at the network level (+0.042, 95% CI [-0.0006, +0.1154]; 97% of draws positive) [dev13]. The empirical edge over the station-by-horizon mean is concentrated at longer horizons: per-horizon network Spearman was 0.932/0.916/0.865/0.659 for the empirical predictor versus 0.938/0.915/0.843/0.603 for r6 at 7/30/90/180 days, so r6 was equal or better at 7 and 30 days and the empirical curve gained at 90 and 180 days [dev13]. On the first panel's direct 858 units the two were effectively indistinguishable (network difference +0.002, 95% CI [-0.024, +0.026]) [dev13].

Against the simple-descriptor model on the same 874 units, the paired network-level difference was large and excluded zero (+0.552, 95% CI [0.309, 0.814]; pooled +0.098 [0.059, 0.142]), and the empirical predictor won the within-network rank comparison in 41 of 57 networks (0.719); the simple model reached pooled 0.846 but only network-level 0.248 on these units [dev13]. The hierarchical surface reached pooled 0.898 and network 0.689 on the direct units; the network x horizon mean reached pooled 0.902 but only network 0.763 [dev13].

Across all 1,446 units, the empirical predictor's network-level rank fell to 0.715 (pooled 0.740), essentially tied with the station-by-horizon mean at the network level (r6 network 0.726; paired difference -0.011, 95% CI [-0.035, +0.008]) and below the simple descriptors in pooled ordering (0.835), because the 596 network-mean fallback rows are constant within a network and destroy within-network ordering [dev13]. The full-panel comparison is therefore an artifact-driven diagnostic; the same-unit paired comparison at directly supported horizons is the paper's claim, and its conclusion is **persistence of local recovery difficulty**—the empirical curve ranks future loss at least as well as the strongest fitting-record baseline, not an order-of-magnitude improvement over it (Figure 2).

### 3.2 Support, duration, interpolation, and extrapolation

The direct-horizon ranking is overwhelmingly carried by exact station x duration x season curves: 841 of 874 units (96.2%) had exact local support, and that tier alone reaches network Spearman 0.887 and pooled 0.968 [dev13]. The station-duration tier held only 9 units (2 networks) and the network-duration tier 0 units in the second panel; 24 additional direct-horizon units fell back to the network mean, so the fallback tier totals 596 units (572 horizon-unsupported plus 24 direct-horizon without a station curve), correcting the earlier count of 572. In the fallback tier, network Spearman fell to 0.562 and pooled to 0.339, and within-network rank is undefined by construction. Support quality degrades monotonically with distance from the exact cell: distance-0 units reach network Spearman 0.931, station-level (distance < 100) 0.629, and network-level 0.759 [dev13].

The continuous support-aware risk surface repairs most of the fallback deficit but not the extrapolation boundary [dev13]. On the second panel its full-panel pooled Spearman was 0.893 (versus 0.740 for the frozen predictor), R2 0.475 (0.238), and RMSE 1.096 C (1.320). On the 572 previously unsupported horizon units it raised network Spearman from 0.597 to 0.846, pooled from 0.388 to 0.879, and R2 from -0.03 to 0.38. The 448 interpolated 14- and 60-day units were well calibrated (pooled Spearman 0.774, calibration slope 1.025), in contrast to the constant fallback (0.451 and 0.509). The 365-day horizon is the hard boundary: rank on the 124 extrapolated units was 0.270 and 90% interval coverage was 46.8% despite the pre-specified widening, against overall coverage of 92.5% (direct 96.0%, interpolated 98.2%). Abstaining the 124 extrapolated units (8.6% of units carrying 28.9% of total loss) raised released-unit network Spearman to 0.691 and R2 to 0.663; extrapolation abstention is therefore the required policy at unsupported durations (Figure 3). Pure-transfer predictions are underdispersed (calibration slopes 1.3–2.3), so absolute magnitudes require external recalibration.

### 3.3 Within-network and temporal stability

The network-level 0.805 is not an artifact of persistent network difficulty: a predictor that perfectly separates network means reaches pooled Spearman of only 0.326 in-sample, while the empirical predictor's network-demeaned pooled Spearman is 0.936 on the direct subset and its median within-network Spearman is 0.965 [dev13]. The ranking lives inside stations and horizons, not between networks. It is stable in time: moving the outer cutoff from 60 to 70 to 80% of each record reproduced network Spearman of 0.984, 0.944, and 0.911 (implementation B, placement-level pooling; the 60% leg attrites to 13 networks because early years lack complete donor rosters), with predicted network ranks agreeing across cutoffs with Kendall's W of 0.811; an independent implementation (A) on supported station-gap cells obtained 0.947, 0.922, and 0.949 with W = 0.917 (14 networks), the same qualitative conclusion under a different pooling scope [dev13]. History length matters: network Spearman rose from 0.608 with 2 years of fitting history to 0.872 at 4 years, 0.916 at 6, 0.938 at 8, and 0.944 with the full record, implying a minimum usable fitting history of roughly 4 years. The concern that the stress model trains on less record than the deployed recovery model (49% versus 70% of years) is empirically negligible: paired MAE difference 0.013 C and pooled Spearman 0.989 between the two stress curves on the same cells [dev13].

### 3.4 Decision experiments: fixed-budget triage negative and coverage-floored selection

**Triage (reported negative).** For fixed-budget prioritization on the full second panel, the frozen empirical predictor is the worst non-random policy [dev13]. At a 20% budget, CapturedLoss was 0.512 (95% CI [0.485, 0.537]) for the simple-descriptor model, 0.504 for duration-plus-season, 0.500 for the surface, 0.500 for raw gap length, and 0.338 ([0.302, 0.380]) for the empirical predictor (random 0.200, oracle 0.529); NDCG@20% was 0.908 for simple and 0.617 for empirical. The paired bootstrap differences were -0.174 ([-0.198, -0.140]) for empirical minus simple and -0.012 ([-0.031, +0.003]) for surface minus simple. The failure is structural: the network-mean fallback tier under-ranks the 365-day loss mass (mean prediction 1.33 C versus observed 5.27 C), which carries 28.9% of total loss. Abstention does not rescue loss-capturing budgets—abstaining the 124 extrapolated units removes 8.6% of units carrying 28.9% of total loss—but it is justified for point-release decisions, where the same abstention raises released-unit network Spearman to 0.691 and R2 to 0.663.

**Model selection.** Without abstention the risk-based selector adds nothing over a development-chosen fixed family or global blocked CV (regret 0.084–0.085 versus 0.0815; per-network average CV, an in-sample benchmark, reaches 0.038) [dev13]. With abstention the picture reverses, but only at low coverage: when every candidate family has unit-level fitting-period stress support and ambiguity abstention is applied (lambda = 0.5, 10% ambiguity threshold), 91.5% of units are abstained, leaving 123 units (8.5% of the panel) from 8 of 42 networks, where the selector's network-balanced regret drops to 0.0067 (95% CI [0.0019, 0.0120]), an order of magnitude below every comparator on the same released units (best fixed 0.151, global CV 0.151, per-network CV 0.164, gap-length rule 0.145, random 0.341). Ambiguity abstention alone does not help—near-ties are cheap; the sharp lever is the support rule. The 8 networks are the t05 stress-covered subset, not a random sample, so the released-unit result is a **proof of concept conditional on those networks**, not a deployable claim: v13 requires coverage floors (>= 50% of units, >= 60% of networks) and a protocol-v4 preregistered evaluation before selection can be claimed (Figure 4).

### 3.5 Downstream thermal outcomes under three untreated baselines

Two implementations, two baselines, one ambiguous story [dev13]. Against the **no-fill default** (implementation B, 15 networks, 351 station-gaps, 1,755 placements), reconstruction reduces integrated-metric distortion substantially: reconstructed error is 12.4% of no-fill error for annual mean, 13.8% for degree days, and 12.6% for trend slope; 30.1% for the 90th percentile, 37.5% for amplitude, and 34.0% for days above 20 C. Single-event metrics are barely distorted at all (reconstruction errors equal to 82–84% of no-fill for summer maximum and days above 25 C), and reconstruction restores computability: no-fill leaves amplitude undefined in 20.9% of placements and reconstruction always returns a value. The fitting-period risk score predicts distortion of the integrated metrics at the network level (annual mean 0.764, degree days 0.743, phase 0.729, 90th percentile 0.668) but not of amplitude (0.089) or summer maximum (0.250).

Against the **climatology default**, the picture inverts for threshold and extreme metrics. In implementation A (1,965 placements, 131 stations), the top-20% risk policy's aggregate distortion is *worse* than the climatology baseline for degree days (-17.9%), days above 20 C (-22.2%), days above 25 C (-42.5%), amplitude (-33.8%), and summer mean (-23.0%); only annual mean is slightly better (+2.2%); gap-length selection behaves similarly. Implementation B's climatology comparison agrees: mean reconstruction error is roughly equal to climatology for annual mean (0.093 versus 0.105 C), degree days (21.7 versus 23.5 degree-days), and days above 20 C (2.23 versus 2.24 d), and worse for days above 25 C (0.516 versus 0.411 d) and summer maximum (0.124 versus 0.096 C). The mechanism is a cold-peak bias: on the long summer gaps that dominate the top-risk budget, the reconstruction is systematically cold at the seasonal peak (negative signed errors; at 90-day gaps, -24.5 degree-days and -0.97 exceedance days), so it flips more threshold crossings than the climatology fill removes. The incremental-benefit reading is therefore: B = D(default) - D(model) is clearly positive for integrated metrics under the no-fill default, near zero or negative for threshold-extreme metrics under the climatology default, and any decision claim must state which default and which metric class it targets (Figure 5). The two implementations' panels differ (1,965 versus 1,755 placements; 131 versus 117 stations), so no pooled downstream number is quoted; harmonization is required before any single benefit estimate can be reported.

### 3.6 Model-family boundaries: cross-instance transfer

Recoverability difficulty is shared within architecture families but is pipeline-specific across them [dev13]. Within the engineered-feature block (linear/PCHIP boundary, seasonal-boundary ridge, donor ridge, XGBoost), self-transfer network Spearman was 0.91–0.98 and cross-transfer within the block 0.72–0.98: the XGBoost stress curve predicted the outer losses of the boundary and ridge families almost as well as its own. Overall, the diagonal (mean 0.783) exceeds the off-diagonal (mean 0.434; one-sided Mann-Whitney p = 0.033), but that gap is driven entirely by the neural and process rows: inside the engineered block the off-diagonal cells are nearly saturated. **Relabeled finding**: the cell previously called BiLSTM "self-transfer" (0.29–0.69 across granularity conventions) is **cross-instance transfer**—the source is a newly trained, properly regularized mask-aware BiLSTM (12 networks, three seeds) and the target is the older frozen bounded-run sensitivity predictions, so no cell in the matrix is a same-instance self-transfer for the neural family. The neural family's cross-transfer to the engineered block was -0.24 to +0.28, and its fitting-period stress correlated only 0.067 with the XGBoost stress on the same networks—the two families disagree about which networks are hard. The air2stream-equivalent process model showed weak cross-instance transfer (0.64 on 8 networks, 14 units) and null-to-negative transfer from XGBoost (0.24 on 8 networks, 89 units). Cells resting on 4–10 networks are fragile and reported with their counts.

### 3.7 Missingness mechanisms: two implementations, no settled claim

Stress curves are mechanism-specific instruments in principle, but the evidence is not yet coherent: two independent implementations disagree on the headline values [dev13]. Implementation A (12 networks, 573–609 units per mechanism) reported matched network Spearman of 0.531 (uniform), 0.944 (multi-block), 0.594 (summer-biased), 0.580 (high-temperature-biased), 0.979 (donor-synchronous), 0.881 (target-plus-primary-covariate), and 0.930 (online left-boundary), with matched calibration slopes 0.89–1.01, and a mismatch experiment in which the uniform-block curve applied to support-destroying mechanisms collapsed rank (donor-synchronous 0.979 to 0.294, target-plus-primary-covariate 0.881 to 0.196, online 0.930 to 0.399, under-predicting loss by 1.1–2.3 C). Implementation B (a different set of 12 networks—only six in common—with 306 station-gaps per mechanism) reported matched network Spearman of 0.944 (uniform), 0.888 (multi-block), 0.909 (summer-biased), 0.958 (high-temperature-biased), **0.490 (donor-synchronous)**, 0.937 (target-plus-air-temperature forcing outage), and 0.965 (online), and found that applying the uniform-block curve to donor-synchronous gaps did *not* collapse rank (0.524 mismatched versus 0.490 matched), while the reverse direction collapsed (donor-synchronous curve on uniform gaps: 0.098). The two implementations differ in panel composition, forcing definitions, and scoring details, and the conflict is unresolved: A's donor-synchronous matched value of 0.979 and B's 0.490 cannot both characterize the same estimand. Until the mechanism definitions and panels are harmonized, the missingness matrix is reported in Supporting Information only, and **no matched-transfer value from either implementation may be cited as evidence**. The seasonal-placement-bias direction (the mildest mismatch) is the only qualitatively consistent finding across implementations. The analytic conditional-covariance operator, whose estimand is an expected Gaussian MAE rather than a variance or lower bound, saturates with gap length while realized loss continues to grow (mean conditional SD 0.475 to 0.565 C and expected Gaussian MAE 0.379 to 0.451 C across 7 to 365 days, versus realized MAE 0.544 to 4.719 C), and its incremental value after the simple model is negligible (linear R2 increment 0.017; learned error model 0.732 to 0.743); this analysis is moved to Supporting Information [dev13].

## 4. Discussion

Four judgments structure what the evidence supports.

First, **historical error persistence is real.** A fitting-period stress curve built with the intended recovery model ranks that model's later loss on wholly unseen networks at directly supported horizons (pooled 0.945, network 0.805), it does so within networks rather than between them (network-demeaned pooled 0.936; median within-network 0.965), and the ordering is stable across rolling-origin cutoffs and fitting histories of at least four years. This is the paper's positive result, and it licenses the practical corollary: a network's risk profile must be scored station by station and horizon by horizon, not read off a network mean.

Second, **the incremental value beyond station-by-horizon history is modest.** The station-by-horizon historical mean—the strongest comparator a manager already possesses without any new machinery—nearly matches the empirical curve in pooled ordering (0.942 versus 0.945), the two predictors correlate 0.992, and the paired network-level difference (+0.042) has a 95% CI that straddles zero. The empirical curve's genuine advantages are concentrated at 90- and 180-day horizons and in within-network station-season detail; claims of large gains from seasonal stratification or from the empirical machinery itself are not supported. Novelty therefore rests on prospective validation, the support hierarchy, model conditionality, mechanism matching, and the decision interface—not on rank-magnitude superiority.

Third, **prediction does not automatically translate into intervention value.** The frozen predictor ranks future error well at supported horizons yet is the worst non-random fixed-budget triage policy (CapturedLoss@20% 0.338 versus 0.512 for simple descriptors): high predicted MAE does not imply high treatment benefit, because the units with the largest loss mass (365-day gaps) sit in the unsupported fallback tier and are under-ranked, and because choosing a unit by predicted error is not the same as choosing a unit whose recovery will reduce downstream distortion. The downstream evidence sharpens this: reconstruction clearly beats the no-fill default for integrated thermal metrics, but under a climatology default it can worsen threshold-extreme metrics on long summer gaps (cold-peak bias), so the same predictor that ranks recovery error well cannot be claimed, without qualification, to protect every ecological metric. Decision value is a property of the pair (predictor, default, metric class), not of the predictor alone.

Fourth, **support and model matching determine when decisions can be released.** Interpolated durations are well calibrated, extrapolated durations fail and must be abstained (365-day rank 0.270, 90% coverage 46.8%), and the abstention that is justified for point release (8.6% of units, 28.9% of loss) is not justified for loss-capturing budgets. Difficulty ordering transfers across engineered-regression families but not across architecture families, so selection instruments must carry per-family curves; and the missingness matrix, which would determine when a curve built under one outage geometry may be applied to another, is presently too inconsistent across implementations to constrain practice. Model selection with per-unit support and abstention reaches regret 0.0067, but only at 8.5% coverage on 8 networks, which is a proof of concept with coverage floors still to be met.

Limitations remain. The direct-support ranking claim is strong only where the exact station-season tier exists (841 of 874 units in the second panel); the station- and network-duration tiers are nearly empty in that panel, the network-mean fallback is weak, and the 365-day horizon is unusable without abstention. The surface was tuned on four fit durations, its absolute calibration requires external recalibration, and its 365-day widening was insufficient. The model matrix rests on 12 networks for the neural row and 8 for the process row; the air2stream equivalent replaces the original optimizer and joins differing daily time conventions. The missingness matrix is not yet harmonized; downstream metrics were scored on 15 networks with one reconstruction family and two non-identical implementations. Every revision analysis reuses already-scored panels; none is independent confirmation.

## 5. Conclusions

Historical block stress tests, built and applied model-conditionally, provide a valid screen for future stream-temperature recovery error *within its support*. On the outcome-disjoint second panel, direct-support pooled Spearman was 0.945 and network-level 0.805, carried by exact station-season curves (0.887) and reflecting within-network station-horizon ordering that persists across horizons, cutoff choices, and fitting histories of at least four years. The signal is one of persistence of local recovery difficulty: the station-by-horizon historical mean nearly matched the empirical curve (pooled 0.942; paired network difference +0.042, CI straddling zero), so the seasonal and empirical increment is modest rather than dominant. The boundaries are as important as the signal: interpolated durations are well calibrated; the 365-day horizon is extrapolation that fails (rank 0.270, 90% coverage 46.8%) and must be abstained; difficulty ordering transfers across engineered-regression families but not across architecture families (the neural self-transfer cell is cross-instance transfer); and the missingness-mechanism matrix is unresolved between two implementations and is not yet a claim. Decision use is conditional: the frozen predictor is the worst non-random fixed-budget triage policy (CapturedLoss@20% 0.338), model selection with support and ambiguity abstention achieves regret 0.0067 only at 8.5% coverage on 8 networks and awaits coverage floors, and downstream benefit is positive for integrated metrics under a no-fill default but negative for threshold-extreme metrics under a climatology default. The present evidence therefore supports model-conditional screening tools with explicit support checks and calibrated abstention; it does not support automatic filling, station removal, or unconditional intervention. Independent preregistered validation—a third, outcome-disjoint panel scored under protocol v4 with fixed coverage floors, a decision-value primary endpoint, and external registration—is required before any of these instruments can be deployed.
