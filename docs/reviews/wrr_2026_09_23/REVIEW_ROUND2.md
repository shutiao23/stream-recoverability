# Referee report, second round — the v17 manuscript

**Manuscript:** *Outage timing, not outage length, decides whether a
stream-temperature year can be used* (`paper_archived/v17/`).

**Review round:** second adversarial read, 2026-09-23. The first round
(`REVIEW.md`) rejected the v16 submission and specified the redesign; this
round reads the result of that redesign with the same hostility, on the
principle that the author of a redesign is the worst possible judge of it.

**Basis:** the manuscript, the claim matrix, the frozen criterion, and my own
re-running of the pipeline. Unlike the first round I could execute the code, so
several of the findings below are experiments rather than objections.

**Recommendation: Major revision.** The core result survives everything I threw
at it, and two of the attacks strengthened it. But I found two reproducibility
defects, one of which invalidated the artifact chain; an unfulfilled promise in
the methods; two dangling references; one framing problem that changes what the
paper should claim as its contribution; and one calibration claim that does not
hold as stated. All are addressable, and all were addressed in the revision
recorded in `RESULTS.md`; this report is the version that found them.

---

## 1. Major comment — the artifact chain was not reproducible, and this is not a
software nit

I regenerated the case table and compared it with the committed one. They
differed: 51,543 rows against 51,495, with the donor-regime composition shifted
(rich 30,965 against 30,898).
The cause is that outage realizations were seeded from Python's built-in
`hash()` of a tuple containing strings, and `hash()` for `str` is salted per
interpreter process. Every outage placement in the study was therefore drawn
from a distribution that changes between runs, and no number in the manuscript
could be regenerated by a reader.

I want to be precise about why this matters here and is not a code-review
remark, which the author has said is out of scope. The experimental unit of
this study *is* the outage realization. If the realizations cannot be
regenerated, then the population on which every claim is evaluated cannot be
regenerated, and the confirmation — which is supposed to be a single, frozen,
once-only test — is not a fixed object at all. A referee cannot distinguish
"the reserved panel gave −6.9 points" from "one draw of the reserved panel gave
−6.9 points and the author reported the draw they saw."

The fix is a deterministic digest, and the whole pipeline must be re-run behind
it. I verified the repaired table is now bit-identical across independent runs
(51,524 rows both times; maximum absolute difference across every numeric
column: 0.0). All results in the revised manuscript must come from the re-run,
and the regression test that pins this must be in the suite rather than in a
comment.

A second, smaller instance of the same disease sits next to it. The case
builder writes its summary to a fixed filename regardless of which output it
was asked to produce, so running the sensitivity analysis silently overwrote
the main run's case count. I caught this only because the count in the numbers
file did not match the count in the parquet. Any script that can be invoked
with different outputs must name its side outputs accordingly, and the
manuscript's numbers should be extracted from the primary artifacts rather than
from summary files that a later run can clobber.

## 2. Major comment — a promise in the methods with no result behind it

Section 2.3 of the submitted draft states that estimators are fit
leave-one-year-out within the station and that "a strictly-prior-years fit is
reported as a sensitivity." No such sensitivity appeared anywhere in the
manuscript, the artifacts, or the code. The protocol matters: leave-one-year-out
lets a 2015 outage be repaired using 2016–2026 data, which is legitimate for
offline archive repair and is the stated use case, but it is the more generous
of the two protocols and it is the one that produces the headline. A reader is
entitled to see the stricter one.

This is the most serious category of defect a referee can find in a methods
section, because it is indistinguishable from an analysis that was run and not
reported. I do not believe that is what happened here — the code contained no
such branch — but the author cannot expect a reader to make that distinction.

I required it implemented and run. Under the stricter protocol the panel falls
from 40,704 to 33,633 development cases and from 4,045 to 3,337 station-years,
reconstruction error rises from 0.242 °C to 0.270 °C, and the headline contrast
moves from −6.23 points (−6.97, −5.47) to **−5.79 points (−6.58, −4.80)**.
Conformal coverage is 0.907, marginally better than under the main protocol.
The claim survives, at a cost of about seven per cent of the effect. This
should have been in the first draft and it is now §3.10.

## 3. Major comment — the contribution is the axis, not the model, and the paper
is framed the wrong way round

I ablated the certificate, re-running the full out-of-fold pipeline for each
variant on the development panel. At 70% retention, against the incumbent
length rule's consequential-error rate:

| screen | rate | points vs incumbent |
|---|---:|---:|
| outage-length rule (incumbent) | 9.23% | — |
| **three-variable logistic regression** | **5.53%** | **−3.70** |
| peak-overlap alone, no model at all | 5.63% | −3.59 |
| three-feature gradient boosting | 5.03% | −4.20 |
| geometry and timing only (8 features) | 5.04% | −4.18 |
| drop donor features | 3.84% | −5.38 |
| drop the observed-peak margin | 3.17% | −6.05 |
| full certificate | 3.01% | −6.21 |

A plain logistic regression on three variables — peak-window overlap,
warm-season coverage, outage length — captures 60% of the total benefit, and
peak-window overlap *by itself, with no fitting at all*, captures 58%.

I then fitted that three-variable rule on the development networks and applied
it unchanged to the reserved panel, exactly as the certificate is applied. It
gives 4.16% against the length rule's 9.49%, a contrast of **−5.33 points (95%
network-clustered interval −6.69 to −3.89)**, and it cuts the rate of
underestimating a site's peak by more than a degree from 1.93% to 0.62%. The
three-variable rule alone clears the effect size the study pre-registered for
its full method.

The author should read this as good news and rewrite accordingly. The paper's
contribution is the demonstration that the screening axis used by published
guidance is close to the weakest usable one, and that a better axis is visible
at decision time and costs nothing to compute. That is a finding an agency can
act on next week. The certificate adds a further 2.5 points and, more
importantly, the calibrated interval — which no simple rule can supply — but it
is the second-order part of the story and it is currently written as the first.
A version of this paper that leads with "stop screening on gap length, screen on
whether the gap covers the site's warm peak" and offers the certificate as the
refinement will be read and used far more widely than one that leads with a
conformalized gradient-boosting machine.

I required the three-variable rule to be reported as a deliverable, with its
coefficients, so that the paper is usable by someone who will never run the
code. It is now in `results/v17_fitness/step16_simple_rule/rule.json`:

    risk = logistic( -1.302 + 2.070 · peak_overlap
                            - 2.412 · warm_observed_fraction
                            - 0.00187 · longest_run_days )

with peak_overlap the share of the station's climatological hottest-30 window
that the outage hides and warm_observed_fraction the share of May–September
days still observed.

One caution the author must attach to this, because I nearly misread it myself.
The coefficient on outage length is *negative*, and it is tempting to write
that longer outages are safer once timing is accounted for. That is not what it
means. Length and warm-season coverage are strongly collinear, coverage carries
almost all of the shared signal, and the small negative weight is what is left
over. The stratified table settles the question directly: holding peak overlap
at zero, the consequential-error rate still rises with length, from 0.6% at
1–30 days to 8.6% at 121–200 days. Length matters; it is simply a much weaker
and much more confounded axis than the one guidance currently uses. Report the
coefficient with that caveat, or drop the length term — it is worth 0.1 points.

## 4. Major comment — I tried to show the incumbent was handicapped, and failed

The obvious attack on §3.3 is that the length rule looks bad because it was
paired with a weak reconstruction, so I paired it with every reconstruction in
the study, including one that cannot be deployed at all: an oracle that picks
the best estimator for each case with perfect hindsight. At 70% retention on
the reserved panel:

| screen | reconstruction | rate above 0.5 °C |
|---|---|---:|
| length rule | information-conditioned policy (the paper's) | 9.68% |
| length rule | always donor ridge, else climatology | 9.90% |
| length rule | always smooth climatology | 15.42% |
| length rule | **oracle over methods, not deployable** | **4.32%** |
| **certificate** | **deployable estimators it chooses itself** | **2.50%** |

The certificate, using only estimators anyone can run, beats the incumbent
screen armed with a reconstruction nobody can run: −1.82 points, 95%
network-clustered interval −2.37 to −0.82 (development −1.70, −2.14 to −1.03).
The reconstruction the paper used is also the strongest of the deployable
pairings, not the weakest, so the comparison was not tilted.

This result should be in the manuscript. It is the cleanest available answer to
the first question any referee will ask, and the author has it and did not
report it.

## 5. Major comment — the ecologically meaningful number is buried

The manuscript reports the rate of error above 0.5 °C and says the frontier is
reported across tolerances. It is not; one tolerance is shown. When I extracted
the full sweep, the number that matters most to the stated motivation was
sitting unreported. At 70% retention on the reserved panel:

| screen | > 0.25 °C | > 0.5 °C | > 1.0 °C | **underestimate > 1 °C** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| length rule | 17.10% | 9.64% | 3.39% | **1.97%** |
| peak-overlap rule | 9.18% | 4.21% | 1.19% | 0.66% |
| certificate | 7.14% | 2.50% | 0.32% | **0.25%** |
| random | 24.32% | 14.43% | 5.35% | 3.54% |

A thermal archive that *understates* a site's peak by more than a degree is the
failure mode that matters for cold-water habitat, because it turns a stressful
year into an unremarkable one. The certificate reduces that rate eightfold
relative to the incumbent. That belongs in the abstract, not in a CSV.

## 6. Minor but substantive

**6.1 The air-driven branch is described but never reported.** The methods call
it a coverage-limited sub-study and the limitations repeat that, but no result
for it appears. When I extracted the numbers, NASA POWER forcing exists for
2.3% of development cases and **0.85% of reserved cases**, and the certificate
selects the air estimator for 0.07% of reserved cases. The honest statement is
that the air branch is essentially absent from the confirmation panel. Say
that, or drop the branch.

**6.2 The certificate uses linear interpolation far more than the information
rule does, and it is right to.** On the reserved panel the certificate selects
interpolation for 25.0% of cases against 5.7% for the information-conditioned
rule, and interpolation carries the lowest error of any choice when selected
(0.098 °C). This is an interpretable finding — for short outages with both
flanks, interpolation beats donor regression — and it explains where the
method-selection component of the gain comes from. It should be reported rather
than left in an artifact.

**6.3 Conditional coverage within networks — and this one does not fully hold.**
The manuscript reports coverage within pre-declared strata, which pools across
networks. Conformal validity assumes an exchangeability that fails between
river networks, so I computed coverage inside each reserved network separately.
The mean is 0.900 and the median 0.905, which is reassuring, but the tenth
percentile is 0.839, the minimum is 0.709, one network of 36 falls below 0.80
and six fall below 0.85. The nominal 90% interval is therefore an honest
statement about a population of networks and is **not** a guarantee at a single
new site.

The author must say this in the results rather than leave it to a reader to
discover, and should not describe the intervals as conditionally valid without
that qualification. A cluster-aware calibration — conformalising on
network-level scores, or a jackknife+ over networks — is the obvious next step
and should be named as open rather than glossed. I regard this as the clearest
remaining methodological weakness in the paper, and I would not accept a
version that asserts site-level coverage.

**6.4 The oracle curve in Figure 2 is an oracle over retention only**, holding
the reconstruction fixed, while the certificate curve also chooses the method.
The curves are therefore not quite commensurable at the left edge. The
difference is small — method selection is worth about 0.2 points — but the
caption should say which oracle it is.

**6.5 Figure captions carried a hardcoded case count** that a re-run had
invalidated. Compute them from the artifacts. More generally, the manuscript
should carry a machine-readable block of its headline numbers that a test
checks against the artifacts, in the way this repository already does for
evidence status. Prose drifts from results; tests do not.

## 7. What I am not objecting to

The pre-registration is genuine and the precision calculation is the right one.
The stratified reservation fixes the defect that invalidated the previous
round. The decomposition by outage arm in §3.4, which discloses that the
absolute effect is regime-dependent and that the freeze failed to declare an
arm weighting, is the kind of self-reporting that should be standard and almost
never is. The negative trend result in §3.5 is reported rather than buried. The
two donor control experiments are well designed and answer questions the
previous programme left open. None of this needs changing.
